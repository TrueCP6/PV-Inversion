\section{Introduction}

The intersection of applied mathematics and atmospheric science provides a rigorous framework for modelling complex meteorological phenomena.
This work introduces foundational atmospheric concepts -- such as atmospheric stratification, potential vorticity, stratospheric intrusions, and cut-off lows -- from an applied mathematics perspective, with a specific focus on their dynamics and significant weather impacts in South Africa.

The theoretical core of this project is governed by the quasigeostrophic potential vorticity (QGPV) equation \cite{Hoskins1985}.
We specify the mathematical formulation of the QGPV equation, detailing its inherent limitations, physical assumptions, and the requisite background states.
To approximate solutions to this equation, we design and implement an efficient numerical solver.
This solver is designed to scale effectively in parallel computational environments and remain computationally tractable for time-stepping, with its mathematical correctness rigorously verified.

A primary objective of this research is to construct an idealised model of stratospheric intrusions caused by cut-off lows, building directly upon the foundational work of Fehlmann \cite{fehlmann1997pvelements}, Sprenger \cite{sprenger2007userguide}, and Barnes et al. \cite{Barnes2022main}.
We aim to replicate their time-independent experiments to validate our computational approach, subsequently comparing results and conducting additional numerical experiments to expand on their findings.
Finally, this project investigates the computational feasibility of time-stepping the QGPV equation.
By implementing time-dependent simulations, we seek to explore the time-evolution and dynamic lifecycle of these idealised atmospheric systems.

\section{Atmospheric Science Concepts}
For readers who are not familiar with atmospheric science, but who are familiar with Applied Mathematics, a brief overview of terminology and atmospheric properties is given.

\subsection{Layers of the Atmosphere}
The atmosphere is divided into a number of distinct layers, a characteristic referred to as \textbf{stratification}. We will focus on the two layers closest to the Earth's surface: the troposphere and the stratosphere. The boundary between these two layers is known as the \textit{tropopause} \footnote{Italicised text is used to show terms that will be defined in more detail later on in the text.} \cite{Vallis2017}.

\begin{itemize}
    \item The \textbf{troposphere} extends from the Earth's surface ($z=0$) up to the tropopause. This layer is mostly transparent to incoming solar (shortwave) radiation, meaning it is primarily heated from below by terrestrial (longwave) radiation emitted by the Earth's surface. This bottom-up heating results in a negative vertical temperature gradient ($\frac{\partial T}{\partial z} < 0$) \cite{Vallis2017}. As a practical example, as you climb higher up a mountain, the temperature tends to decrease with increasing altitude. The warmer air close to the surface is less dense than the colder air at higher altitudes, which leads to convective instability \cite{danielson2003meteorology}. Consequently, this layer experiences high turbulence \cite{sprenger2007userguide} and substantial dissipation of energy; almost all typical weather phenomena occur here.

    \item Above the troposphere lies the \textbf{stratosphere}. This layer contains a higher concentration of ozone, which absorbs high-energy ultraviolet (UV) solar radiation, heating the layer from within. Solar radiation first heats the ozone at higher altitudes, losing energy as it penetrates deeper into the lower stratosphere. This results in a positive temperature gradient ($\frac{\partial T}{\partial z} > 0$) \cite{Vallis2017}. Because warmer, less dense air sits atop cooler, denser air, the stratosphere is highly stable with respect to vertical convection \cite{Barnes2022main}.

    \item The \textbf{tropopause} serves as the boundary between the negative temperature gradient of the troposphere and the positive temperature gradient of the stratosphere. At this boundary, the temperature gradient experiences a discontinuity \cite{Panchev1985-et}. The altitude of the tropopause primarily varies with latitude ($\varphi$), occurring at an altitude of about \qty{16}{\km} in equatorial regions and dropping to approximately \qty{8}{\km} in polar regions \cite{Vallis2017}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Terminology}
The meaning of certain relevant terms that will be used later on is given. We assume that the reader is familiar with the quantities temperature ($T$), pressure ($p$), density ($\rho$) and acceleration due to gravity ($g$).

\begin{itemize}
    \item An \textbf{adiabatic} process involves a system exchanging no heat with its surroundings. In the case of the movement of an air parcel, the process is adiabatic if temperature only changes due to changes in pressure \cite{Vallis2017}.
    
    \item The \textbf{specific gas constant for dry air} ($R$) is \qty{287.05}{\joule\per\kg\per\kelvin}. It is used to relate $p$, $\rho$ and $T$ using the bulk ideal gas law $p=\rho RT$ \cite{Vallis2017}.

    \item The \textbf{specific heat capacity of dry air} ($c_p$) is the amount of heat necessary to heat up \qty{1}{\kg} of dry air by \qty{1}{\kelvin}. We will take it to be \qty{1005}{\joule\per\kg\per\kelvin}

    \item
    \textbf{Potential temperature} ($\theta$) is the temperature an air parcel would attain if it were brought adiabatically to some reference pressure $p_0$.
    $p_0$ is usually taken to be \qty{1000}{\hecto\pascal} \cite{sprenger2007userguide}.
    
    \begin{equation}\label{pot_temp}
        \theta = T \left( \frac{p_0}{p}\right)^{R/c_p}
    \end{equation}

    In a purely adiabatic process potential temperature is conserved.

    \begin{equation}\label{pot_temp_conserved}
        \frac{D\theta}{Dt} = \frac{\partial \theta}{\partial t} + \bm{u} \cdot \nabla \theta = 0
    \end{equation}

    \item
    The \textbf{Brunt–Väisälä frequency} ($N$) is a measure of the stability of an air parcel to vertical displacements, such as those caused by convection \cite{sprenger2007userguide}.
    The stratosphere tends to have larger values of $N^2$ than the troposphere \cite{Barnes2022main}, representing greater stability and less turbulent flow.
    
    \begin{equation}\label{brunt_vaisala}
        N^2 = \frac{g}{\theta} \frac{\partial \theta}{\partial z}
    \end{equation}

    \item \textbf{Relative vorticity} ($\bm{\omega}$) is defined as the curl of the wind velocity vector, i.e. $\bm{\omega} = \nabla \times \bm{u}$. It physically represents the local spin of a fluid element relative to the Earth's surface \cite{Vallis2017}. 

    \item The \textbf{Coriolis parameter} ($f$) is a local measure of the effect that the Earth's rotation has on the movement of fluids (such as the atmosphere) on the Earth's surface \cite{Vallis2017}. It is calculated as $f = 2 |\Omega| \sin (\varphi)$, where $\varphi$ is latitude, and $\bm\Omega$ is the angular momentum of the Earth \cite{sprenger2007userguide}. By convention $f$ is negative in the Southern Hemisphere \cite{Barnes2022main}.

    \item The \textbf{zonal} and \textbf{meridional} directions refer to the west-to-east and south-to-north directions respectively. This text will use the convention that the $x$-axis points east and the $y$-axis points north.

    \item \textbf{Hydrostatic equilibrium} is an assumption often made to simplify large-scale atmospheric models. It presumes that the downward gravitational force on a fluid parcel is precisely balanced by the upward force due to the pressure gradient. It can be written as \cite{Vallis2017}:

    \begin{equation}\label{hydrostatic_equilibrium}
        \frac{\partial p}{\partial z} = - \rho g
    \end{equation}

    \item
    \textbf{Geostrophic} describes a flow in which the force induced by horizontal pressure gradients is exactly balanced by the Coriolis force \cite{WMO1992}.
    This causes the resulting geostrophic wind to blow parallel to contours of constant pressure.
    This balance holds approximately for large-scale weather system in the mid latitudes, as long as the the \textit{Rossby number} is assumed to be small \cite{Vallis2017}.

    \item 
    \textbf{Geostrophic vorticity} ($\zeta_g$) is the vertical component of the vorticity of geostrophic wind, measuring the rotation about the vertical axis \cite{HOLTON_chapter6,WMO1992}.
    It is quantitatively defined as follows, where $\psi_g$ is the geostrophic stream function \cite{Vallis2017}.

    \begin{equation}\label{geostrophic_vorticity}
        \zeta_g = \bm{\hat k} \cdot \bm{\omega_g} = \frac{\partial v_g}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial u_g}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial ^2 \psi_g}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial ^2 \psi_g}{\partial y^2} 
    \end{equation}

    \item The \textbf{Rossby number} ($\mathrm{Ro}$) is the ratio of the typical flow speed $U$ to $f$ times the typical length scale $L$.
    A typical value in the mid-latitude atmosphere is $\mathrm{Ro} \sim 0.1$.
    The natural scale for $\zeta_g$ is $U/L$, so the Rossby number can be approximated as \cite{HOLTON_chapter6,Vallis2017}:

    \begin{equation}\label{rossby_number}
        \mathrm{Ro} = \frac{U}{fL} \approx \left| \frac{\zeta_g}{f}\right|
    \end{equation}

    \textit{Quasigeostrophic theory} is only formally valid when $\mathrm{Ro} \ll 1$, but remains qualitatively useful despite this restrictive condition \cite{HOLTON_chapter6}.
    
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Potential Vorticity}
In one of his most significant contributions, Ertel showed that under adiabatic and frictionless conditions, there exists a conserved quantity in the idealised atmosphere.
We will call this quantity Ertel's potential vorticity (EPV) and denote it as $Q$ \cite{fehlmann1997pvelements,ertel_english}:

\begin{equation}\label{epv}
    \frac{DQ}{Dt} = \frac{D}{Dt} \left( \frac{(\bm{\omega} + 2 \bm{\Omega}) \cdot \nabla \theta}{\rho} \right) =0
\end{equation}
where the material derivative $\frac{D}{Dt} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t} + \mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla$ is used and $Q$ has dimensions of $M^{-1} L^2 T^{-1} \Theta$ \cite{Barnes2022main}.

\subsection{Rossby Waves}\label{sec:rossby_waves}

\textbf{Rossby waves} are the large scale meanders (wave lengths $\sim \qty{3000}{\kilo\meter}$) of the mid-latitude prevailing westerly winds, manifesting in the upper troposphere as a series of alternating troughs and ridges \cite{Stull2015} along the jet stream and the tropopause \cite{Hoskins1985}.
They owe their existence to a background gradient of PV, most simply that provided by the variation of $f$ with latitude;
The potential vorticity of an air parcel displaced towards the pole or equator is conserved, so according to \cref{epv}, its relative vorticity ($\bm{\omega}$) may change to compensate for the change in planetary vorticity ($2 \bm{\Omega}$) \cite{Vallis2017}.
The induced circulation pushes parcels back towards their original latitude, but a quarter wavelength out of phase with the displacement, so the pattern propagates westward relative to the mean flow.
In terms of potential vorticity, a Rossby wave is a wavy displacement of PV contours on a surface of constant $\theta$ (isentropic surface).
The sharp PV gradient at the tropopause tends to act as a waveguide along which these waves travel \cite{Hoskins1985, Stull2015}.

At small amplitude these undulations are reversible: the PV contours are displaced, but return to their original positions once the wave has passed.
As the amplitude grows, however, the contours no longer simply undulate but overturn, and the wave breaks.
\textbf{Rossby wave breaking} is therefore a strongly nonlinear and irreversible deformation of the PV contours, analogous to the breaking of ocean waves on a beach \cite{Hoskins1985,Vallis2017,NdaranaWaugh2010}.
During equatorward breaking, tongues of high-PV stratospheric air are drawn equatorward and downward, appearing as a tropopause fold in the tropopause in a vertical cross-section \cite{Sprenger2007ste,Hoskins1985,Stull2015}.
These filaments can detach entirely from the main stratospheric reservoir of high-PV air, forming pools of high-PV air surrounded by low-PV air in the troposphere \cite{Sprenger2007ste}.

\subsection{Cut-off Lows}\label{sec:cols}

When such a filament detaches, the isolated high-PV anomaly induces a closed cyclonic\footnote{Clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere and anticlockwise in the Northern Hemisphere; the sense in which geostrophic wind flow circulates around a low pressure zone \cite{WMO1992}.} circulation beneath it that is cut off equatorward from the prevailing westerlies: a cold-cored \textbf{cut-off low} (COL) \cite{WMO1992}.
Rossby wave breaking is the dominant formation mechanism: 89\% of Southern Hemisphere COLs are linked to RWB events, with most of the remainder linked to PV intrusions in which high-PV air is advected equatorward without the contours overturning \cite{NdaranaWaugh2010}.
With a more generous spatial and temporal search window, 99.3\% of COLs are found to be co-located with at least one RWB event \cite{Barnes2021col}.

COLs differ widely in vertical extent, and fall largely into two groups.
Shallow COLs are confined to the upper troposphere and fade out around the \qty{500}{\hecto \pascal} level\footnote{Corresponds to an altitude of $\approx \qty{5.5}{\kilo\meter}$ \cite{Stull2015}} or higher, whereas deep COLs extend all the way to the surface, and make up 58\% of all COLs.
Very few COLs reach 700 to \qty{925}{\hecto \pascal} without reaching the surface, warranting the pseudo-binary distinction between deep and shallow COLs.
Shallow COLs are most common in the subtropics, while deep COLs dominate poleward of \ang{30}S in the extratropics.
Over the southern African sector only 49\% of COLs reach the surface.
Shallow COLs are most frequent in summer \cite{Barnes2021col}, when the tropopause is higher \cite{SeidelRandel2006}, whereas deep COLs peak in autumn \cite{Barnes2021col}.

The distinction matters because only deep COLs are accompanied by a surface cyclonic circulation, adding strong winds, large waves and storm surge to the heavy rainfall that COLs of all depths can produce \cite{Barnes2021col,NdaranaWaugh2010}.
In South Africa, COLs contribute 16\% of the total annual rainfall on the Cape south coast, and deep moist convection within them produces short bursts of extreme rainfall responsible for 20\% of all flash-flooding events \cite{Barnes2021col}.

On its western flank, a deep COL draws cold polar air equatorward \cite{Barnes2021col, NdaranaWaugh2010, SingletonReason2007}.
This dense air raises the surface pressure and contributes to a ridging high pressure zone behind the low \cite{Barnes2021col}.
The tight pressure gradient between the two drives moist air onshore, nearly perpendicular to the coast \cite{Ndarana2018, Barnes2021capestorm}, against the steep coastal topography of South Africa, where it is forced to rise \cite{SingletonReason2007}.
Combined with the COL's own cyclonic forcing, this uplift can produce extreme rainfall  \cite{Barnes2021capestorm,SingletonReason2007}.
Some COLs are quasi-stationary \cite{NdaranaWaugh2010}, and the topography itself can hold the associated surface low in place \cite{SingletonReason2007}, so that these conditions persist over the same region for several days \cite{Barnes2021col}.

The high-PV air at the heart of a COL is a \textbf{stratospheric intrusion}: highly stable stratospheric air drawn down sloping isentropic surfaces into the troposphere \cite{Hoskins1985, Stull2015, Barnes2022main}.
Stratospheric air has high PV mainly because of its strong static stability \cite{Hoskins1985, Grise2010}, and in the absence of diabatic and frictional effects it conserves this PV as it descends \cite{Stull2015}.
Within the intrusion the PV anomaly is realised partly as increased static stability and partly as increased cyclonic vorticity, the balance depending on its shape \cite{Hoskins1985}.
Its influence, however, extends beyond the anomaly itself: the circulation it induces penetrates vertically, and beneath it the isentropes bow apart, so that the weakly stratified air below trades static stability for cyclonic rotation \cite{Hoskins1985}.
The circulation induced by an intrusion that reaches low enough can extend all the way to the surface \cite{Barnes2022main}, where it advects the latitudinal temperature gradient, creating a warm anomaly ahead of the intrusion that induces a cyclonic circulation of its own \cite{Hoskins1985}.

Potential vorticity ties these processes together through the \textbf{invertibility principle}: given a suitable balance condition, the distribution of PV on isentropic surfaces together with $\theta$ on the lower boundary determines all other dynamical fields (wind, pressure, temperature, static stability) \cite{Hoskins1985}.
From this point of view, a COL can be understood as the interaction of two anomalies \cite{Barnes2021col}.
The stratospheric intrusion is a cyclonic (in the Southern Hemisphere, negative) upper-level PV anomaly \cite{Barnes2022main}, and a warm surface potential-temperature ($\theta$) anomaly is equivalent to a cyclonic PV anomaly concentrated at the ground \cite{Hoskins1985}.
Each induces cyclonic circulation of its own, and when the warm surface anomaly lies just downstream (east) of the upper anomaly, their circulations reinforce one another and the two anomalies amplify together \cite{Hoskins1985, Barnes2021col}.
The depth of the stratospheric intrusion is therefore important in determining the depth of the COL, but it is not the whole story: the surface $\theta$ anomaly must also be in phase with the upper anomaly.
An out-of-phase surface anomaly can inhibit the COL from extending to the surface: only 12\% of shallow COLs have an in-phase surface anomaly when they form, compared with 66\% of deep COLs \cite{Barnes2021col}.
In 53\% of deep COLs with a closed surface low (57\% over southern Africa), the surface cyclone develops at or after the formation of the COL rather than pre-existing, and the two generally develop almost simultaneously \cite{Barnes2021col}.
Once formed, a COL over a warm ocean can generate a surface $\theta$ anomaly of its own through surface heat fluxes, coupling the surface to the upper-level anomaly and allowing the system to intensify \cite{SingletonReason2007, Barnes2021col}.

\section{Objectives}

As discussed above, COLs and Rossby wave breaking are responsible for a substantial share of the extreme weather experienced over and around South Africa.
Both are associated with stratospheric intrusions: tongues of high-PV air that extend deep into the troposphere and induce a circulation that can reach the surface.

The primary aim of this project is to quantify how the structure of a stratospheric intrusion, and the background atmosphere in which it is embedded, determine its surface response, and hence its capacity to produce severe weather.

The approach rests on the PV invertibility principle.
Inverting the full Ertel PV (\cref{epv}) is a difficult numerical problem because of its strong nonlinearity.
We therefore use the
\textit{quasigeostrophic} approximation, in which the inversion reduces to a linear elliptic problem for the geostrophic streamfunction (\cref{qgpv}).
This is considerably more tractable, though still non-trivial, while retaining the essential dynamics of an intrusion.

The specific objectives of this project are to:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item recreate the idealised background atmospheric conditions of the control state used by Barnes et Al. \cite{Barnes2022main}

  \item develop and validate a numerical solver for the QGPV inversion problem (\cref{qgpv}) for idealised intrusions embedded in a prescribed background state;
  
  \item optimise this solver, both for use on consumer-grade hardware and a high-performance computing (HPC) cluster, so that large parameter sweeps become computationally feasible;

  \item assess the limitations of the quasigeostrophic PV framework to determine which results are physically meaningful
  
  \item use systematic parameter sweeps to isolate the effect of individual intrusion and background-state parameters on the surface fields, and to characterise the relationship between derived quantities, such as the minimum height of the dynamical tropopause, and the strength of the surface response;
  
  \item implement an optimisation scheme that automatically searches parameter space for the intrusions and background states that produce the most severe surface response; and
  
  \item integrate these configurations forward in time using the prognostic \cref{qgpv_time} to assess their longevity and evolution.
\end{enumerate}

This text therefore focuses on solutions to \cref{qgpv,qgpv_time} and their physical implications.
It builds on the PV inversion framework of Fehlmann~\cite{fehlmann1997pvelements} and Sprenger~\cite{sprenger2007userguide},
and on the South African COL work of Barnes et~al.~\cite{Barnes2022main}.

\section{The Quasigeostrophic Potential Vorticity Equation}

\subsection{Derivation}

A summarised derivation from \cref{epv} to \cref{qgpv} is given, noting assumptions and terms dropped along the way.
Throughout, synoptic scaling is assumed, with Burger number $\mathrm{Bu} \equiv \left(\overline N H / f_0 L\right)^2 = \mathcal{O}(1)$ \cite{Vallis2017}, and orders of magnitude are stated relative to the leading-order (background) contribution to $Q$.

The full absolute vorticity in \cref{epv} is given by \cite{HOLTON_chapter2}:

\begin{equation}
    \bm{\omega} + 2 \bm{\Omega} = \left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial v}{\partial z}, \quad 2 |\Omega|\cos\varphi+\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}-\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}, \quad f + \frac{\partial v}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} \right)
\end{equation}

At synoptic scales ($U \sim 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $W \sim 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $L \sim 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m}$, $H \sim 10^{4}\,\mathrm{m}$) \cite{HOLTON_chapter2}, $|\partial_y w / \partial_z v | \sim |\partial_x w / \partial_z u | \sim (W/U)(H/L) \sim 10^{-5}$, so the horizontal gradients of $w$ may be neglected in the vorticity.
Furthermore, $\frac{2 |\Omega|\cos(\varphi)\, \partial_y \theta}{f\, \partial_z \theta} \sim \cot\varphi \, \frac{H}{L} \, \frac{\mathrm{Ro}}{\mathrm{Bu}} \sim 10^{-2} \mathrm{Ro}$, allowing the $\cos$ term to be discarded as well, consistent with the traditional approximation \cite{HOLTON_chapter2,Vallis2017}.
The resulting approximation is:

\begin{equation}
    \bm{\omega} + 2 \bm{\Omega} \approx \left(-\frac{\partial v}{\partial z}, \quad \frac{\partial u}{\partial z}, \quad f + \frac{\partial v}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} \right)
\end{equation}

Making a change of variables using the hydrostatic assumption $\partial p /\partial z = - \rho g$ (\cref{hydrostatic_equilibrium}) and substituting into \cref{epv} yields EPV in pressure coordinates \cite{Hoskins1985}, where $\nabla _p = (\partial_x, \partial_y, \partial_p)$ and $\bm{u} = (u, v, 0)$ is the horizontal velocity.
Once hydrostatic balance is assumed this change of variables is exact: the extra terms arising from $(\partial_x)_z \to (\partial_x)_p$ \cite{Vallis2017} cancel identically.

\begin{equation}\label{epv_pressure}
\begin{aligned}
    Q &= -g \left[ \left(f + \zeta_p\right)\frac{\partial \theta}{\partial p} - \frac{\partial v}{\partial p} \left(\frac{\partial \theta}{\partial x}\right)_p + \frac{\partial u}{\partial p} \left(\frac{\partial \theta}{\partial y}\right)_p\right] \\
    &= -g (f \bm{\hat k} + \nabla_p \times \bm{u} ) \cdot \nabla_p \theta
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{zeta_p}
    \zeta_p \equiv\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\right)_p - \left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right)_p
\end{equation}

It is assumed that $\theta$ can be split into two components: a horizontally uniform `reference potential temperature profile' $\overline \theta(p)$, and the potential temperature anomaly $\theta^*$ \cite{Hoskins1985,HOLTON_chapter6}.

\begin{equation}
    \theta(x,y,p) = \overline \theta (p) + \theta^* (x,y,p)
\end{equation}

The anomaly is assumed to be weak in the sense that its vertical gradient is small compared with the reference stratification, $\partial_p \theta^* / (d\overline\theta/dp) = \mathcal{O}(\mathrm{Ro})$, which holds for geostrophically balanced anomalies when $\mathrm{Bu} = \mathcal{O}(1)$ \cite{Vallis2017}.
The density and geopotential are split in the same way, $\rho = \overline\rho + \rho^*$ and $\Phi = \overline\Phi + \Phi^*$, and the reference buoyancy frequency $\overline N$ is defined from $\overline\theta$ by \cref{brunt_vaisala}.
Substituting into \cref{epv_pressure} yields the following without further approximation (cf.\ the analogous height-coordinate expansion of \cite{Vallis2017}):

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    Q &= -g\Bigg[\underbrace{f\,\frac{d \overline{\theta}}{d p}}_{\mathcal{O}(1)}
+\underbrace{\zeta_p\frac{d \overline{\theta}}{d p}+f\frac{\partial\theta^*}{\partial p}}_{\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{Ro})}
+\underbrace{\zeta_p\frac{\partial\theta^*}{\partial p}
-\frac{\partial v}{\partial p}\frac{\partial\theta^*}{\partial x}
+\frac{\partial u}{\partial p}\frac{\partial\theta^*}{\partial y}}_{\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{Ro}^2)}\Bigg] \\
&= -g \frac{d\overline{\theta}}{d p} \left[f + \zeta_p + \frac{f}{\partial_p \overline{\theta}} \frac{\partial \theta^*}{\partial p} \right] + \mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{Ro}^2\right)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The term $\zeta_p \partial_p \theta^*$ is the product of two $\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{Ro})$ quantities.
The last two terms can be shown to be $\mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{Ro}^2\right)$ using thermal wind balance \cite{Vallis2017}.
To do so we introduce the function $\mathcal{R}(p)$, where $\Pi(p) = c_p \left(p/p_0\right)^{R/c_p}$ is the Exner function \cite{Hoskins1985}:

\begin{equation}\label{R_p_def}
    \mathcal{R}(p) = \frac{d\Pi}{dp} = \frac{R \Pi(p)}{c_pp} = \frac{RT}{p \theta} = \frac{1}{\rho \theta}
\end{equation}

In pressure coordinates the full geopotential is defined as $\Phi (x,y,p) = g \cdot z(x,y,p)$.
Combining $d \Phi = g \,dz$, \cref{hydrostatic_equilibrium} and \cref{R_p_def} gives hydrostatic equilibrium in terms of the geopotential \cite{HOLTON_chapter6,Hoskins1985}:

\begin{equation}\label{hydrostatic_geopotential}
    \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial p} = - \frac{1}{\rho} = - \mathcal{R}\theta
\end{equation}

Since $\mathcal{R}$ depends only on $p$, this holds separately for the reference state and for the anomalies, $\partial_p \Phi^* = -\mathcal{R}\theta^*$ \cite{Hoskins1985}.
Combining with geostrophic balance, $(u_g, v_g) = f_0^{-1}\left(-\partial_y \Phi^*, \partial_x \Phi^*\right)$ \cite{HOLTON_chapter6}, gives the thermal wind relations $f_0 \partial_p v_g = -\mathcal{R}\, \partial_x \theta^*$ and $f_0 \partial_p u_g = \mathcal{R}\, \partial_y \theta^*$ \cite{HOLTON_chapter6}, so that

\begin{equation}
    -\frac{\partial v}{\partial p}\frac{\partial\theta^*}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial u}{\partial p}\frac{\partial\theta^*}{\partial y} \approx \frac{f_0}{\mathcal{R}} \left|\frac{\partial \bm{u}_g}{\partial p}\right|^2
\end{equation}

Relative to the leading term $f_0\, d\overline\theta/dp$, and using \cref{minus_R_dtheta} below, this has magnitude \cite{Vallis2017}

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\left|\partial_p \bm{u}_g\right|^2}{\left|\mathcal{R}\, d\overline\theta/dp\right|} = \frac{\left|\partial_z \bm{u}_g\right|^2}{\overline N^2} = \mathrm{Ri}^{-1} \sim \frac{\mathrm{Ro}^2}{\mathrm{Bu}}
\end{equation}

Invoking the $\beta$-plane approximation for the Coriolis parameter $f \approx f_0 + \beta y$ \cite{HOLTON_chapter6} allows a further $\mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{Ro}^2\right)$ term to be separated out using the assumption that $\beta L / f_0 \in \mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{Ro}\right)$ \cite{Vallis2017}.

\begin{equation}
    Q = -g \frac{d\overline{\theta}}{d p} \left[f + \zeta_p + \frac{f_0}{\partial_p \overline{\theta}} \frac{\partial \theta^*}{\partial p} \right] - \underbrace{g \beta y \frac{\partial \theta^*}{\partial p}}_{\mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{Ro}^2\right)}  + \mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{Ro}^2\right)
\end{equation}

giving the further approximation

\begin{equation}\label{let_A_B}
    Q \approx A(p)B, \qquad A(p) \equiv -g \frac{d \overline{\theta}}{dp} > 0, \qquad B \equiv f + \zeta_p + f_0 \frac{\partial \theta^*}{\partial p} \left( \frac{d \overline{\theta}}{dp}\right)^{-1}
\end{equation}

where $A > 0$ for a statically stable reference state \cite{HOLTON_chapter2}.

The invertibility principle concerns the distribution of PV on surfaces of constant $\theta$ (isentropic surfaces) \cite{Hoskins1985}, so we make the change of variables of the height coordinate from $p$ to $\theta$, without approximation, using the exact formula \cite{Vallis2017}

\begin{equation}\label{change_coords}
    \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}\right)_\theta = \left(\frac{\partial Q }{\partial x}\right)_p + \frac{\partial Q }{\partial p}\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}\right)_\theta
\end{equation}

First we calculate $(\partial_x p)_\theta$ on the isentrope $(\partial_x \theta)_\theta = 0$.

\begin{equation}
    0 = \left( \frac{\partial \theta ^*}{\partial x}\right)_p + \left(\frac{d\overline{\theta}}{d p} + \frac{\partial \theta^*}{\partial p}\right) \left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}\right)_\theta
\end{equation}

Let $\Delta p_H$ be the depth (in pressure) of the anomaly and $\Delta p_R$ the depth over which the reference stratification changes.
Since $\partial_p \theta^* / \partial_p \overline\theta = \mathcal{O}(\mathrm{Ro})$, we have $\theta^* / \partial_p \overline\theta \sim \mathrm{Ro}\, \Delta p_H$, so the slope of the isentropes is $\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{Ro}\, \Delta p_H / L)$ \cite{Vallis2017}:

\begin{equation}\label{temp_pxtheta}
    \left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}\right)_\theta
    = - \frac{(\partial_x \theta^*)_p}{\partial_p \overline{\theta} + \partial_p \theta^*}
    = - \underbrace{\frac{1}{\partial_p \overline{\theta}} \left( \frac{\partial \theta^*}{\partial x}\right)_p}_{\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{Ro}\, \Delta p_H / L)} \left[1 + \mathcal{O}(\mathrm{Ro})\right]
\end{equation}

The $\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{Ro})$ relative correction in square brackets may be dropped.
We write

\begin{equation}\label{b_is_O_ro}
    b \equiv \frac{B-f_0}{f_0} = \frac{\beta y}{f_0} + \frac{\zeta_p}{f_0} + \frac{\partial _p \theta^*}{\partial _p \overline\theta} = \mathcal{O}(\mathrm{Ro})
\end{equation}

And hence $B \equiv f_0(1+b)= f_0(1+\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{Ro}))$.
Apply the product rule:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial Q}{\partial p} = A'(p) B + A(p)\frac{\partial B}{\partial p} =  \underbrace{f_0 A'}_{\boxed{1}} + \underbrace{f_0 A' b}_{\boxed{2}} + \underbrace{A \frac{\partial B}{\partial p}}_{\boxed{3}}
\end{equation}

We have $\partial_p B \sim \mathrm{Ro}\, f_0 / \Delta p_H$ and $A' \sim A / \Delta p_R$.
Each term enters \cref{change_coords} multiplied by $(\partial_x p)_\theta \sim \mathrm{Ro}\, \Delta p_H / L$, and must be compared with the leading term $(\partial_x Q)_p = A\, \partial_x B = A f_0\, \partial_x b \sim A f_0 \mathrm{Ro} / L$:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \frac{\boxed{1} \, (\partial_x p)_\theta}{A\, \partial_x B} &\sim \frac{f_0 A}{\Delta p_R} \, \frac{\mathrm{Ro}\, \Delta p_H}{L} \, \frac{L}{A f_0 \mathrm{Ro}} = \frac{\Delta p_H}{\Delta p_R} \\
    \frac{\boxed{2} \, (\partial_x p)_\theta}{A\, \partial_x B} &\sim \mathrm{Ro}\, \frac{\Delta p_H}{\Delta p_R} \\
    \frac{\boxed{3} \, (\partial_x p)_\theta}{A\, \partial_x B} &\sim A \frac{\mathrm{Ro}\, f_0}{\Delta p_H} \, \frac{\mathrm{Ro}\, \Delta p_H}{L} \, \frac{L}{A f_0 \mathrm{Ro}} = \mathrm{Ro}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Terms $\boxed{2}$ and $\boxed{3}$ are therefore $\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{Ro})$ relative to the leading term, i.e.\ $\mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{Ro}^2\right)$ overall, for anomalies of any depth, and are dropped.
Term $\boxed{1}$ is retained: it is comparable with the leading term when the anomaly depth is comparable with the scale of the reference stratification, and becomes negligible only for shallow anomalies ($\Delta p_H \ll \Delta p_R$), in which case the result below reduces to its Boussinesq form \cite{Hoskins1985,HOLTON_chapter6}.
To the order required,

\begin{equation}\label{dQ_dp}
    \frac{\partial Q}{\partial p} \approx f_0 A' = -g f_0 \frac{d^2 \overline \theta}{dp^2}
\end{equation}

Combining with \cref{temp_pxtheta} yields:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \frac{\partial Q}{\partial p} \left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}\right)_\theta &\approx \left( -g f_0 \frac{d^2 \overline \theta}{dp^2}\right)\left[- \frac{1}{\partial_p \overline{\theta}} \left( \frac{\partial \theta^*}{\partial x}\right)_p \right] \\
    &= \frac{g f_0 \partial_p^2 \overline\theta}{\partial_p \overline\theta} \frac{\partial \theta ^*}{\partial x} = A(p) \left [-\frac{ f_0 \partial_p^2 \overline\theta}{\left(\partial_p \overline\theta\right)^2} \frac{\partial \theta ^*}{\partial x}\right]
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Because $A$ only depends on $p$,

\begin{equation}
    \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} \right)_p = A(p) \left(\frac{\partial B}{\partial x}\right)_p
\end{equation}

Substituting into \cref{change_coords} gives:

\begin{equation}\label{dQ_dz_theta}
    \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}\right)_\theta \approx A(p) \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)_p \Bigg[ f + \zeta_p + \underbrace{f_0 \frac{\partial _p \theta^*}{\partial _p \overline\theta} -\frac{ f_0 \theta ^*\partial_p^2 \overline\theta}{\left(\partial_p \overline\theta\right)^2}}_{ \displaystyle f_0 \frac{\partial}{\partial p}\left(\frac{\theta^*}{\partial_p \overline\theta}\right)}\Bigg]
\end{equation}

Applying the same process in the $y$ direction (where $\partial_y f = \beta$ is retained), we define the absolute quasigeostrophic potential vorticity $\tilde q$ in pressure coordinates as follows, relating to $Q$ by \cref{epv_to_q_pressure} \cite{Hoskins1985}.

\begin{equation}\label{qgpv_pressure}
    \tilde q \equiv f + \zeta_p + f_0 \frac{\partial }{\partial p} \left[ \theta^* \left(\frac{d \overline \theta}{d p}\right)^{-1}\right]
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{epv_to_q_pressure}
    \nabla_\theta Q \approx -g \frac{d \overline \theta}{d p} \nabla _h \tilde q
\end{equation}

Here $\nabla_\theta \equiv \left((\partial_x)_\theta, (\partial_y)_\theta\right)$ and $\nabla_h \equiv \left((\partial_x)_p, (\partial_y)_p\right)$ are the horizontal gradients along isentropic and isobaric surfaces respectively \cite{Hoskins1985}.
The relation holds only for these horizontal components: the vertical derivative $\partial Q / \partial p$ is dominated by the background PV gradient $f_0 A'(p)$, which has no counterpart in $\partial \tilde q / \partial p$.

We define the stream function as $\psi=\Phi^* / f_0$, a scaling of the geopotential anomaly $\Phi^*$, so that the geostrophic wind is $(u_g, v_g) = (-\partial_y \psi, \partial_x \psi)$ \cite{Hoskins1985,Vallis2017}.
Applying \cref{hydrostatic_geopotential} to the anomaly fields and rearranging we get \cite{Hoskins1985}

\begin{equation}\label{theta_star_pressure}
    \theta^* = - \frac{f_0}{\mathcal{R}} \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial p}
\end{equation}

Approximating the relative vorticity by its geostrophic value, $\zeta_p \approx \zeta_g = \nabla_h^2 \psi$ (\cref{geostrophic_vorticity}), which introduces a relative error of $\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{Ro})$ \cite[p.~181]{HOLTON_chapter6}, and substituting \cref{theta_star_pressure} into \cref{qgpv_pressure} gives us QGPV in terms of the stream function \cite{Hoskins1985,HOLTON_chapter6}:

\begin{equation}\label{qgpv_pressure_psi}
    \tilde q = f + \nabla_h^2 \psi + f_0^2 \frac{\partial}{\partial p} \left( \frac{-1}{\mathcal{R} \partial_p \overline\theta} \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial p}\right)
\end{equation}

We apply hydrostatic equilibrium (\cref{hydrostatic_equilibrium}) once more using the reference density $\overline{\rho}(z)$\footnote{This is a simplification that makes the derivation more compact, but not as correct as that of Hoskins et al. In the full derivation a new density $\rho_s$ and potential temperature $\theta_0$ are introduced, where $\rho_s / \overline\rho = \overline\theta/\theta_0$. In addition $z$ becomes a pseudo-height coordinate defined by $z = g^{-1} \theta_0 \left[\Pi(p_0) - \Pi(p) \right]$, as opposed to the true geometric height \cite{Hoskins1985}.}:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{d \overline \theta}{dp} = \frac{d \overline{\theta}}{dz} \frac{d z}{d p} = - \frac{1}{\overline\rho g} \frac{d \overline{\theta}}{dz}
\end{equation}

Combine with \cref{brunt_vaisala} and \cref{R_p_def} \cite{Hoskins1985}:

\begin{equation}\label{minus_R_dtheta}
    - \mathcal{R}  \frac{d \overline \theta}{dp} = \frac{1}{g^2 \overline\rho^2} \frac{g}{\overline\theta} \frac{d \overline\theta}{dz} = \frac{\overline{N}^2}{g^2 \overline\rho^2}
\end{equation}

And finally substitute into the last term of \cref{qgpv_pressure_psi}:

\begin{equation}\label{qgpv_last_term}
\begin{aligned}
     f_0^2 \frac{\partial}{\partial p} \left( \frac{-1}{\mathcal{R} \partial_p \overline\theta} \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial p}\right) &= f_0^2 \frac{\partial}{\partial p} \left( \frac{g^2 \overline\rho^2 }{\overline{N}^2} \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial p}\right) \\
     &= f_0^2 \left(\frac{- 1}{g \overline\rho} \frac{\partial }{\partial z}\right) \left [ \frac{g^2 \overline\rho^2 }{\overline{N}^2} \left(\frac{- 1}{g \overline\rho} \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial z}\right)\right] \\
     &= \frac{f_0^2}{\overline{\rho}} \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \left( \frac{\overline{\rho}}{\overline{N}^2}  \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial z} \right)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Now we can at last put \cref{qgpv_pressure_psi} and \cref{qgpv_last_term} together and define the relative quasigeostrophic potential vorticity $q \equiv \tilde q - f$ \cite[p.~6]{sprenger2007userguide} to yield the main equation of study by Barnes et al.\ \cite{Barnes2022main} (cf.\ \cite{Vallis2017}):

\begin{equation}\label{qgpv}
    \boxed{ q(x,y,z,t) = \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial y^2} + \frac{f_0^2}{\overline{\rho}(z)} \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \left( \frac{\overline{\rho}(z)}{\overline{N}(z)^2}  \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial z} \right) } 
\end{equation}

We impose Neumann horizontal boundary conditions \cite{Barnes2022main,sprenger2007userguide}, which follow from the geostrophic relations $u = -\psi_y$ and $v = \psi_x$ \cite{Barnes2022main}, and which prescribe the boundary-parallel (tangential) wind on each lateral wall:

\begin{equation}\label{qgpv_bcs_hor}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
    \psi_x(0,y, z) &= \psi_x(L_x,y, z) = \overline v(y,z) \\
    \psi_y(x,0, z) &= \psi_y(x,L_y, z) =- \overline u(x,z)
\end{aligned}
}
\end{equation}

Changing \cref{theta_star_pressure} to height coordinates, and using the background state of \cref{R_p_def} ($\mathcal{R} = 1/\left(\overline{\rho} \overline\theta\right)$) yields

\begin{equation}\label{theta_star}
    \frac{f_0}{g \overline\rho} \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial z} = \mathcal{R} \theta^* \quad \implies \quad 
\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial z} = \frac{g\theta^*}{f_0\overline\theta}
\end{equation}

Which we use for our vertical boundary conditions \cite{Barnes2022main}:

\begin{equation}\label{qgpv_bcs_vert}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
    \psi_z(x,y,0) &= \frac{g\theta ^*}{f\overline{\theta}(0)} \\
    \psi_z(x,y,H) &=\frac{g\theta ^*}{f\overline{\theta}\left(H\right)}
\end{aligned}
}
\end{equation}

To derive the prognostic equation for $q$, we change \cref{epv} into isentropic coordinates using the same approach as \cref{change_coords}:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{DQ}{Dt} = \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial t}\right)_\theta + \frac{Dx}{Dt} \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}\right)_\theta + \frac{Dy}{Dt}\left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial y}\right)_\theta + \frac{D \theta}{Dt} \frac{\partial Q}{\partial t} = 0
\end{equation}

It follows from the definition of $D/Dt$ that $Dx/Dt = u$, and $Dy/Dt=v$.
Reapplying our assumption of adiabatic flow (\cref{pot_temp_conserved}) yields $D\theta/Dt = 0$, simplifying the equation to

\begin{equation}\label{Q_conserved_theta}
    \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial t}\right)_\theta + \bm{u} \cdot \nabla_\theta Q =0
\end{equation}

Reusing the logic from \cref{change_coords} through to \cref{dQ_dz_theta} with $x \to t$ gives

\begin{equation}
    \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial t}\right)_\theta \approx A(p) \left( \frac{\partial \tilde q}{\partial t}\right)_p
\end{equation}

Substituting into \cref{Q_conserved_theta} along with \cref{epv_to_q_pressure}, and dividing by $A(p)$ yields

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \tilde q}{\partial t} + \bm{u} \cdot \nabla_h \tilde q = \frac{D \tilde q}{Dt} \approx 0
\end{equation}

For $\beta=0$ this reduces to the final nonlinear transport form of the prognostic equation for $q$:

\begin{equation}\label{qgpv_time}
    \boxed{\frac{\partial q}{\partial t} - \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y} \frac{\partial q}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x} \frac{\partial q}{\partial y} = 0}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Background States and Coefficients}
We aim to recreate the control state of the atmosphere used by Barnes et al. to use as the benchmark for the diagnostic solver (\cref{sec:numerical_soln}).
In order to capture larger jet streams in section TODO, we use an increased vertical domain of $H=\qty{25}{\kilo \meter}$, as opposed to the $H=\qty{20}{\kilo \meter}$ used by Barnes et al. \cite{Barnes2022main}.
The same $f$ as Barnes et al. is used with $\varphi = \ang{-42}$ and $\beta=0$.
A horizontal domain size of $L_x = L_y = \qty{7500}{\kilo \meter}$ is set.

We will specify $\overline{N}(z)$ using the same values as Barnes et al. \cite{Barnes2022main}, from which we can calculate $\overline{\rho}(z)$ using the same methodology as Fehlmann \cite{fehlmann1997pvelements}.

Barnes et al. specify a stability profile such that $\overline N_\text{strat} = \qty{0.03}{\per\second}$ in the stratosphere and $\overline N_\text{trop} = \qty{0.01}{\per\second}$ in the troposphere \cite{Barnes2022main}.
This discontinuity at the tropopause is smoothed out by the following family of kink functions \cite{fehlmann1997pvelements}:

\begin{equation}\label{kappa_delta}
    \kappa_\delta (x) = \begin{cases}
        0 & x \leq 0 \\
        \frac12 (2x)^\delta & 0 < x \leq\frac12 \\
        1-\frac12 [2(1-x)]^\delta & \frac12 < x \leq 1 \\
        1  & x>1
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

where $\delta >1$ is a parameter which determines the 'sharpness' of the kink.
We can then rescale and translate $\kappa_\delta$ to specify our reference Brunt–Väisälä frequency profile, where $z_\text{trop}$ is the altitude of the centre of the tropopause and $w_\text{trop}$ is the total vertical width of the smoothing zone.
Initially we will take $\delta = 2$, $z_\text{trop}=\qty{12500}{\meter}$ \cite{Barnes2022main} and $w_\text{trop}=\qty{1000}{\meter}$ \cite{fehlmann1997pvelements}.

\begin{equation}\label{N_bar}
    \overline{N}(z)=\left(\overline{N}_\text{strat} - \overline{N}_\text{trop}\right) \kappa _\delta \left( \frac{z-z_\text{trop}}{w_\text{trop}} + \frac12\right) + \overline{N}_\text{trop}
\end{equation}

We can then determine the reference potential temperature profile by solving for $\overline{\theta}$ in the definition of the Brunt–Väisälä frequency from \cref{brunt_vaisala},

\begin{equation}\label{theta_bar}
    \overline{\theta}(z) = \overline{\theta}(0) \exp \left( \frac{1}{g} \int _0 ^z \overline{N}(\xi)^2 \, d\xi\right)
\end{equation}

We combine the ideal gas law $p = \rho R T$ and the the definition of $\theta$ (\cref{pot_temp}) to express the reference density profile, where $\kappa = R/c_p$:

\begin{equation}\label{rho_bar}
    \overline{\rho}(z) = \frac{\overline{p}}{R \overline{\theta}} \left( \frac{p_\text{surf}}{\overline{p}}\right)^\kappa
\end{equation}

To determine the pressure profile $\overline{p}(z)$ we use the hydrostatic assumption (\cref{hydrostatic_equilibrium}), substitute in equation \eqref{rho_bar}, and solve the ODE to yield \cite{fehlmann1997pvelements}.

\begin{equation}\label{p_bar}
    \overline{p}(z) = \overline {p}(0) \left( 1- \frac{\kappa g}{R} \left( \frac{p_\text{surf}}{\overline{p}(0)}\right)^\kappa \int_0^z \frac{d \xi}{\overline{\theta}(\xi)}\right)^{1/\kappa}
\end{equation}

It can be numerically calculated that the relative error between $\psi$ when $\overline{p}(0)=\qty{1000}{\hecto \pascal}$ and $\overline{p}(0)=\qty{800}{\hecto \pascal}$ is $\sim 0.01$, which justifies this approximation, and the decision to treat $\overline{p}(0)$ as constant across varying parameter sets.

\subsection{Boundary Conditions}
To specify the horizontal boundary conditions, we need to choose values of $u$ and $v$. We will define a jet stream aiming to recreate the one shown in \cite{Barnes2022main}.
The exact function used is not given, but we can specify a function that also has a maximum magnitude of $u_\text{jet} =\qty{35}{\meter\per\second}$, is centred about the middle of the tropopause vertically, is centred about the middle of the domain in the $y$-direction ($y_\text{jet} = L_y /2$), is uniform in the $y$-direction, has a Gaussian shape, and has a similar overall size to the one shown in~\cite{Barnes2022main} ($L_\text{jet} \approx \qty{500}{\km}$ and $z_\text{jet} \approx \qty{4}{\kilo\meter}$).
We will let

\begin{equation}\label{u_boundary}
    \overline u(y,z) = U_\text{jet} \exp\left[ - \left(\frac{y - y_\text{jet}}{L_\text{jet}}\right)^2 - \left(\frac{z - z_\text{trop}}{z_\text{jet}} \right)^2\right]
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{v_boundary}
    \overline v = 0
\end{equation}

\subsection{Source Term}\label{sec:source_term}

To calculate the source term $q$ we must first specify the background state $\overline q$ and the QGPV associated with the anomaly, $q^*$. 
We integrate \cref{u_boundary,v_boundary} using $\overline u = -\overline \psi_y$ and $\overline v = \overline \psi_x$ to calculate the background stream function $\overline\psi$:

\begin{equation}\label{psi_bar}
    \overline{\psi}(y,z) = -\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2} U_\text{jet}\, L_\text{jet} \operatorname{erf}\left(\frac{y - y_\text{jet}}{L_\text{jet}}\right) \exp \left[- \left(\frac{z - z_\text{trop}}{z_\text{jet}}\right)^2\right]
\end{equation}

The background QGPV $\overline q$ can then be calculated using \cref{qgpv}, with $\overline{\zeta_g} = \nabla^2_h\overline{\psi}$ (\cref{geostrophic_vorticity}):

\begin{figure}[tp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{background_geostrophic_vorticity.pdf}
    \caption{Background geostrophic vorticity calculated from \cref{psi_bar} using \cref{geostrophic_vorticity}}
    \label{fig:zeta_g_bar}
\end{figure}

\begin{equation}
    \overline q = \overline{\zeta_g} + \frac{f_0^2}{\overline{\rho}} \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \left( \frac{\overline{\rho}(z)}{\overline{N}^2}  \frac{\partial \overline\psi}{\partial z} \right)
\end{equation}

Barnes et al. specify the anomaly in EPV, which has different units to QGPV, so the following approximation is used to calculate $\overline{Q}$ \cite{fehlmann1997pvelements}:

\begin{equation}\label{Q_to_q_conversion}
    q \approx \frac{\overline{\rho} g}{\overline{N}^2 \overline{\theta}} Q - f
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{Q_bar}
    \overline{Q} \approx \frac{\left( \overline q + f\right)\overline{N}^2 \overline{\theta}}{\overline{\rho} g}
\end{equation}

We specify the same PV anomaly as the one used by Barnes et Al. \cite{Barnes2022main} bar the apparent typographical errors.
We take $Q^*_\text{mag} = \qty{-4e-6}{\kelvin \meter \squared \per \second \per \kg}$, and $Q^*_\text{clip} = \qty{-1.5e-6}{\kelvin \meter \squared \per \second \per \kg}$.
The control anomaly will be placed at the centre of the domain ($x_\text{ano} = L_x/2$ and $y_\text{ano} = L_y/2$).
The anomaly sizes $x_\text{size} = y_\text{size} = \qty{200}{\km}$ and $z_\text{size} = \qty{2000}{\meter}$ will be used \cite{Barnes2022main}.

\begin{equation}\label{Q_star}
    Q^*(x,y,z) = \operatorname{softmax}_\tau\left\{Q^*_\text{clip}, \enspace Q^*_\text{mag} \exp \left[- \left(\frac{x - x_\text{ano}}{x_\text{size}}\right)^2 - \left(\frac{y - y_\text{ano}}{y_\text{size}}\right)^2 - \left(\frac{z - z_\text{trop}}{z_\text{size}}\right)^2\right]\right\}
\end{equation}

A $\operatorname{softmax}_\tau \{\}$ function is used in place of the $\max\{\}$ function, so as to improve numerical conditioning.
$\operatorname{softmax}_\tau$ is $C^\infty$ -- infinitely smooth for $\tau>0$.
The smoothing value chosen was $\tau=\displaystyle 0.075 |Q^*_\text{mag}|$.

\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{softmax}_\tau \{a,b\} \equiv \max \{a,b\} + \tau \ln \left[1+\exp \left(- \frac{|a-b|}{\tau} \right)\right]
\end{equation}

    \centering
    \includegraphics{epv.pdf}
    \caption{EPV distribution specified in \cref{Q_bar,Q_star}. A contour interval of \qty{0.5}{PVU} (Potential Vorticity Units) is used. $\qty{1}{PVU} = \qty{e-6}{\kelvin \meter \squared \per \second \per \kg}$. The thick solid line represents the dynamical tropopause -- the \qty{-1.5}{PVU} contour. Thin solid lines represent contours where $Q>0$, indicating a non-physical background state.}
    \label{fig:epv_combined}
\end{figure}

EPV inversion comes with the condition that $fQ > 0$ everywhere in the domain; a necessity to ensure \cref{epv} remains elliptic and not ill-posed \cite{Hoskins1985}.
\Cref{qgpv} is elliptic and linear, so the inversion will not fail for $q$ that corresponds to $fQ\leq 0$.
QGPV is however an approximation of EPV in this case \cite{fehlmann1997pvelements}, so the $fQ > 0$ constraint must be checked manually.
As is seen in \cref{fig:epv_combined}, $Q=\overline{Q} + Q^*$ has regions where $fQ \leq 0$, implying an EPV inversion over this atmospheric state would fail.
No explanation is offered as to why Barnes et al. chose a background state that has this feature.
It must however be acknowledged that Barnes et al. did not publish all information necessary to recreate their background state exactly\footnote{Quantities used but not explicitly given by Barnes et al. include but are not limited to $w_\text{trop}$, $\delta$ and $L_\text{jet}$ \cite{Barnes2022main}.}, so whether this holds is unknown \cite{Barnes2022main}.

Nevertheless, we establish a metric of the volume of 'invalid EPV' compared to the total domain volume as a diagnostic:

\begin{equation}\label{V_minus}
    \mathcal{V}_-\equiv\frac{1}{L_xL_yH} \int_V \vartheta(-fQ)\,dV, \qquad
    \vartheta(x) \equiv \begin{cases}
        1 & x >0 \\
        0 &x < 0
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

For a truly correct background state, we need $\mathcal{V}_- =0$, however the control state has $\mathcal{V}_- \sim 0.04$.
To maintain parity with Barnes et al., we will use this background state for now.

Numerical experimentation showed that $\mathcal{V}_-$ is driven largely by $\partial \overline{u} / \partial z$, meaning that the most effective way to send $\mathcal{V}_- \to 0$ is by decreasing $U_\text{jet}$, or increasing $z_\text{jet}$.

Despite these issues, we arrive at the expression for the source term of \cref{qgpv} by using approximation \eqref{Q_to_q_conversion} again:

\begin{equation}\label{full_q}
    q \approx \frac{\overline{\rho} g}{\overline{N}^2 \overline{\theta}} \left(\overline Q + Q^*\right) - f
\end{equation}

\section{Numerical QGPV Inversion}\label{sec:numerical_soln}

By adopting a smoothing value of $\delta = 2$ for \cref{kappa_delta}, the integral in \cref{theta_bar} is of the following general form \cite{Mathematica}:

\begin{equation}\label{nonelementary_integral}
    \int e^{z^4}\,dz = - \frac{z \Gamma \left( \frac{1}{4}, -z^4\right)}{4 \sqrt[4]{-z^4}} + C 
\end{equation}

Because this integral lacks a closed-form solution, the coefficients (\cref{rho_bar}) and the source term (\cref{full_q}) cannot be readily expressed analytically.
Consequently, numerical integration is required, for the vertical integrals in \cref{theta_bar,p_bar}, as well as solving for $\psi$ in \cref{qgpv}.
The solver set-up for \cref{qgpv} is given in the following subsections.

\subsection{Choice of Solver Framework}

While spectral methods offer exponential convergence and efficient memory usage, they trigger non-physical oscillations, known as the Gibbs phenomenon, when applied to localised functions (such as \cref{full_q}) or kink functions (such as \cref{N_bar}) \cite{Trefethen2000}.
Finite difference schemes are straightforward to implement, but require discretising the Neumann boundary conditions (\cref{qgpv_bcs_hor,qgpv_bcs_vert}) \cite{fehlmann1997pvelements}.
Furthermore, although finite volume methods excel at tracking fluxes in hyperbolic conservation laws (such as \cref{qgpv_time}), scaling to higher-order accuracy requires implementing more complex schemes \cite{Durran2010}.
To overcome these limitations, we employ a finite element method (FEM).
FEM handles Neumann boundary conditions in the weak form as a boundary integral \cite{Segal2011femns}, effortlessly scales to higher-order accuracy and supports formulations (such as Discontinuous Galerkin) that strictly enforce flux conservation \cite{FiredrakeUserManual}.

This work will use the Python library \href{https://www.firedrakeproject.org/}{Firedrake} as the computational framework to build up the numerical solver \cite{FiredrakeFirstPaper}.
It allows a high level specification of PDEs, automatic highly optimised C code generation and automatic Jacobian computation.
These features significantly reduce development time, ensuring the project remains within the scope of an honours thesis.
Furthermore, Firedrake provides access to some of the most efficient matrix solvers and precondtioners, such as conjugate gradient and polynomial multigrid, by simply changing a few lines in the solver parameters \cite{FiredrakeUserManual, PETScUserManual, petsc-efficient, Dalcin2011}.

Unfortunately Firedrake does not offer a simple way to evaluate purely vertical integrals (such as \cref{theta_bar,p_bar}) efficiently, so a non-trivial alternative method is explained in full in \cref{app:vert_int}.

\subsection{Building the Mesh}\label{sec:mesh}

To optimize mesh generation, a two-dimensional base mesh is extruded into a three-dimensional domain \cite{McRae2016, Bercea2016}.
This extrusion methodology was found to significantly reduce the computational cost of mesh creation compared to direct three-dimensional mesh initialisation.
The corresponding implementation is detailed in \cref{app:mesh}.

By restricting the base mesh to quadrilateral elements \cite{HomolyaHam2016}, the vertical extrusion produces a mesh composed exclusively of hexahedral cells.
This hexahedral topology facilitates the construction of tensor-product function spaces across the elements \cite{McRae2016}.
Consequently, during form assembly and the application of matrix-free operators \cite{KirbyMitchell2018}, the framework inherently leverages sum factorisation \cite{FiredrakeUserManual, Homolya2017}.
This technique decomposes multi-dimensional integrals into a sequence of one-dimensional operations, thereby reducing the per-cell computational complexity from $\mathcal{O}(p^6)$ to $\mathcal{O}(p^4)$ for matrix-free evaluations \cite{Homolya2017, KirbyMitchell2018}, and from $\mathcal{O}(p^9)$ to $\mathcal{O}(p^7)$ for explicit sparse matrix assembly \cite{Homolya2017}, where $p$ denotes the polynomial degree of the function space.

It should be noted that the asymptotic complexity advantages conferred by sum factorisation during matrix-free matrix-vector multiplications do not fully translate to assembled matrix methods.
For explicitly assembled matrices, these performance improvements are strictly confined to the assembly phase \cite{Homolya2017, KirbyMitchell2018}.

\subsection{Defining the Function Space}\label{sec:cg_space}

Firedrake offers a large number of supported finite elements, each with its own advantages and disadvantages \cite{FiredrakeUserManual}.
Since the unknown stream function is a scalar field and the extruded mesh consists of hexahedral cells, the vector- and tensor-valued elements, as well as those defined only on simplices, are not applicable.
Of the remaining options, the simplest are the continuous and discontinuous Galerkin elements, which on hexahedra are constructed directly as tensor products of one-dimensional interval elements \cite{Homolya2017}; this structure is also what permits sum factorisation.
The continuous Galerkin space is the natural choice for the second-order elliptic inversion problem.
The discontinuous Galerkin space offers greater flexibility, including the representation of discontinuous fields, at the cost of additional interface terms and a penalty parameter that must be chosen sufficiently large to ensure stability \cite{larson2013finite}.
The specific Firedrake variants used for meshes constructed with tensor products are the \mintinline{python}{'Q'} and \mintinline{python}{'DQ'} function spaces \cite{FiredrakeUserManual}.

Equation \eqref{qgpv} is essentially an advection-diffusion equation with a source term.
If the equation is advection-dominated we need to take care that the selected function space does not result in non-physical oscillations.
The \mintinline{python}{'Q'} function space simply handles diffusion, but can result in non-physical oscillations if the flow is advection-dominated \cite{Durran2010}.
The \mintinline{python}{'DQ'} function space allows for stable upwinding and explicit conservation of flux between cells, but introduces a larger number of degrees of freedom for the same mesh and requires a more complex formulation such as the Symmetric Interior Penalty Galerkin (SIPG) method to handle diffusion \cite{FiredrakeUserManual}.

Since there is no advection in the $x$ and $y$ directions in \cref{qgpv}, we expand the vertical derivative term to determine which process dominates:

\begin{equation}\label{advection_order}
    \frac{f_0^2}{\overline{\rho}}\frac{\partial}{\partial z} \left( \frac{\overline{\rho}}{\overline{N}^2}  \psi_z\right) = \psi_z \underbrace{\frac{f_0^2}{\overline{\rho}}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\frac{\overline{\rho}}{\overline{N}^2}}_{C_1(z)} + \psi_{zz} \underbrace{\frac{f_0^2}{\overline{N}^2}}_{C_2(z)}
\end{equation}

We compute the ratio of advection to diffusion:

\begin{equation}\label{adv_diff_ratio}
     \frac{C_1(z)}{C_2(z)} = \frac{\overline{N}^2}{\overline{\rho}}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\frac{\overline{\rho}}{\overline{N}^2} = \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \ln \left( \frac{\overline{\rho}}{\overline{N}^2}\right)
\end{equation}

Numerically calculating the absolute maximum of \cref{adv_diff_ratio} for $z \in [0, H]$ reveals that $|C_1/C_2| < 0.005$, meaning that in the vertical direction \cref{qgpv} is heavily dominated by diffusion. 

It is likely that increasing $p$ will stabilise these oscillations on a 3D mesh (as it does on a 1D and 2D mesh \cite{CAI2014}).
Nevertheless, we calculate a \textbf{heuristic} stability parameter based on the concept of a mesh Péclet number \cite{Donea2003}. Here $h$ is the local mesh spacing.

\begin{equation}\label{peclet_def}
    \mathrm{Pe}= \frac{hC_1}{2C_2}
\end{equation}

For linear ($p=1$) 1D continuous Galerkin formulations, the constraint $\mathrm{Pe} \le 1$ prevents spurious oscillations.
We will adopt this constraint as 'a best guess' of the stability bound of the \mintinline{python}{'Q'} function space, as no oscillation criterion is available for $p>2$ in 3D \cite{Donea2003}.

When calculating $\mathrm{Pe}$ \eqref{peclet_def}, $h$ can not be taken to be constant.
Even though we take $\Delta z$ as constant, Firedrake by default places nodes at Gauss-Lobatto-Legendre (GLL) points.
These points are not equally spaced to improve numerical conditioning \cite{FiredrakeUserManual} and mitigate the Runge phenomenon \cite{Trefethen2000}.
The maximum spacing between GLL nodes for some $p$ is given by \cref{tab:gll_max_spacing}.

Consequently, the maximum spacing of nodes in a cell of size $\Delta z$ can be expressed as $h_\text{max} = \Delta x _\text{max} (p) \Delta z / 2$, and $\mathrm{Pe}$ \eqref{peclet_def} for $p=4$ can be bounded by

\begin{equation}\label{peclet_bound}
    \mathrm{Pe}_{p=4} \leq \frac{h_\text{max}}{2} \left| \frac{C_1}{C_2}\right| <  \frac{\Delta z}{1222}
\end{equation}

We have the strictly heuristic stability constraint $\Delta z < \qty{1222}{\meter}$, however the smallest feature we wish to capture is the smoothed tropopause which has a vertical thickness of $w_\text{trop} = \qty{1000}{\meter}$, so a safer limit would be $\Delta z \leq \qty{500}{\meter}$.

It is shown that a solution using the \mintinline{python}{'Q'} function space will likely not be polluted by spurious oscillations caused by high advection.
Since the solutions to \cref{qgpv} appear to be reasonably smooth \cite{Barnes2022main}, we can take advantage of the high order convergence high degree polynomials provide, using fewer degrees of freedom than a standard 2nd order finite difference scheme, while achieving far greater accuracy \cite{Donea2003} (see \cref{fig:error_convergence}).
We will initially take $p=4$; a relatively arbitrary choice.
The definition with Firedrake is written as

\begin{minted}{python}
p = solver_params.polynomial_order # For now p=4
cg_space = FunctionSpace(mesh, 'Q', p) # Sometimes called 'V'
\end{minted}

Note that the total number of degrees of freedom can be retrieved programatically with \mintinline{python}{V.dim()} \cite{FiredrakeUserManual} or analytically \cite{Homolya2017}:

\begin{equation}\label{degrees_of_freedom}
    \text{DOFs} = (N_x p+1)(N_y p +1 )(N_z p + 1)
\end{equation}

where if $N_x = N_y=N_z=40$ and $p=4$, the total number of degrees of freedom is $\approx 8 \times 10^6$.
Barnes et al.\ solve the inversion with the finite-difference scheme of Sprenger \cite{sprenger2007userguide}, which uses three-point centred differences on a uniform grid \cite{Barnes2022main} and is therefore second-order accurate \cite{LeVeque2007}.
Barnes et al. used a second order finite difference scheme and a total of \num{12e6} mesh points \cite{Barnes2022main}.

\subsection{Weak Form}
To set up \cref{qgpv} to be solvable with the finite element method, we multiply by a test function $\phi$ and integrate over the 3D domain $V$.
Before integrating we also multiply the whole equation by $\overline\rho$, in order to allow for additional optimisations later on.
We use the horizontal Laplace operator $\nabla^2_h=\partial^2_x+\partial^2_y$ to make this process more concise.

\begin{equation}\label{weak_form_1}
    \int_V { \overline\rho \phi q  \,dV} = \underbrace{\int_V {\overline\rho \phi \nabla_h^2 \psi  \,dV}}_{I_h} + f_0^2 \underbrace{\int_V { \phi\frac{\partial}{\partial z} \left( \frac{\overline \rho}{\overline N^2} \psi _z \right) \,dV}}_{I_z}
\end{equation}

Now we apply Green's first identity \cite{kellogg2026foundations}, which can be written as equation \eqref{greens_1st_identity} for a general number of spatial dimensions.
Here $A$ is the surface that encloses $V$.

\begin{equation}\label{greens_1st_identity}
    \int_V {\phi \nabla^2 \psi \,dV} = \int_A {\phi \nabla \psi \cdot \bm {\hat {n}} \,dA} - \int_V {\nabla \psi \cdot \nabla \phi \,dV}
\end{equation}

This yields the following when applied to $I_h$ and $I_z$ \eqref{weak_form_1}.
The horizontal normal vector is denoted as $\bm {\hat n_{xy}} = (n_x, n_y, 0)$.

\begin{equation}\label{i_h}
    I_h = \underbrace{\int_A {\overline\rho \phi \nabla_h \psi \cdot \bm {\hat n_{xy}} \, dA}}_{I_{hb}} - \int_V {\overline\rho\,\nabla_h \psi \cdot \nabla_h  \phi \, dV}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{i_z}
    I_z = \underbrace{\int _A {\frac{\overline \rho}{\overline N^2} \phi \psi_z n_z \, dA}}_{I_{zb}} - \int_V {\frac{\overline \rho}{\overline N^2} \phi_z \psi_z \,dV}
\end{equation}

We can then expand the boundary integrals $I_{hb}$ and $I_{zb}$ to implement the boundary conditions 
(\cref{qgpv_bcs_hor,qgpv_bcs_vert}):

\begin{equation}
    I_{hb} = \int_A {\overline \rho \phi \left( \psi_x n_x + \psi_y n_y\right)\,dA}
    = \int_A {\overline \rho \phi \left( \overline v n_x - \overline u n_y\right)\,dA}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    I_{zb} = \int _A {\phi\frac{\overline \rho}{\overline N^2} \frac{g\theta ^*}{f_0\overline{\theta}} n_z \, dA}
\end{equation}

We then substitute everything into equation \eqref{weak_form_1} and move terms around to get the form $a(\psi,\phi) = L(\phi)$:

\begin{equation}\label{final_weak_form}
    \underbrace{\int_A {\overline \rho \phi \left( \overline v n_x - \overline u n_y\right)\,dA} + f_0^2 \int _A {\phi\frac{\overline \rho}{\overline N^2} \frac{g\theta ^*}{f\overline{\theta}} n_z \, dA} - \int_V { \overline\rho \phi q  \,dV}}_{L(\phi)} = \underbrace{\int_V {\overline\rho\,\nabla_h \psi \cdot \nabla_h \phi \, dV} + \int_V {\frac{ f_0^2\overline \rho}{\overline N^2} \phi_z \psi_z \,dV}}_{a(\psi, \phi)}
\end{equation}

Now \cref{qgpv}, with the boundary conditions embedded, is in a weak form that can easily be expressed in Firedrake's syntax\footnote{shown in \cref{app:weak_form}}.

\begin{figure}[tp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{plots/alpha_sparsity.pdf}

    The mesh is refined as $p$ decreases ($N=8, 4, 2$), so that every matrix has the same $9^3 = 729$ degrees of freedom and thus dimensions.
    Shaded entries are non-zero; nnz/row is the mean number of stored non-zero entries per row, which grows with $p$ as each basis function overlaps with more of its neighbours.}
    \label{fig:alpha_sparsity}
\end{figure}

Establishing certain properties of the matrix arising from $\alpha$ will aid in optimising the numerical solver later on.
We notice that $\alpha$ is symmetric, as $\alpha(\psi, \phi) = \alpha(\phi, \psi)$.
It can be shown that $\alpha$ is positive semi-definite by evaluating

\begin{equation}\label{alpha_phi_phi}
    \alpha(\phi, \phi) = \int_V {\,(\phi_x^2 + \phi_y^2) \overline\rho \, dV} + \int_V {\frac{ f_0^2\overline \rho}{\overline N^2} \phi_z^2 \,dV}
\end{equation}

Since every term in \cref{alpha_phi_phi} is $\geq 0$, it follows that $\alpha (\phi,\phi) \geq 0$ for all real $\phi$.
These properties are transferred to the matrix arising from \cref{final_weak_form} \cite{larson2013finite}.

\subsection{Solvability Condition}\label{sec:solv_cond}

Equation \eqref{qgpv} and its boundary conditions (\cref{qgpv_bcs_hor,qgpv_bcs_vert}) only contain derivatives of $\psi$. This means that if $\psi$ is a solution, $\psi + C$ will also be a solution.
To ensure the weak form \eqref{final_weak_form} is solvable, we calculate $\theta^* \in \mathbb{R}$ such that the constant solution is valid.
This is called the Neumann solvability condition, and it requires that the test function $\phi=C$ satisfies \cref{final_weak_form} \cite{larson2013finite}.
In other words, we solve for $\theta ^*$ in the equation $\alpha(\psi, C) = L(C)$.
As the bilinear form only contains derivatives of the test function, it follows that $\alpha(\psi, C)=0$.
Additionally, $C$ can be pulled out of the integrals in $L(C)$ and divided away, leaving the following solution condition:

\begin{equation}\label{solve_cond_1}
    \oint_A {\overline \rho \left( \overline v n_x -  \overline u n_y\right)\,dA}
    + \underbrace{f_0^2\int _A {\frac{\overline \rho}{\overline N^2} \frac{g\theta ^*}{f\overline{\theta}} n_z \, dA}}_{I_1}
    - \int_V { \overline\rho q  \,dV} = 0
\end{equation}

The integrand of $I_1$ exclusively depends on $z$, meaning the integral can be evaluated directly.

\begin{equation}
    I_1 = \theta ^*L_x L_y f_0 g \left(\frac{\overline \rho (H)}{\overline N(H)^2 \overline \theta(H) } - \frac{\overline \rho (0)}{\overline N(0)^2 \overline \theta(0) }\right)
\end{equation}

Rearranging \cref{solve_cond_1} yields the formula for $\theta^*$:

\begin{equation}\label{general_solv_cond}
    \theta^* = \frac{\displaystyle \int_V { \overline\rho q  \,dV} - \int_A {\overline \rho \left( \overline v n_x - \overline u n_y\right)\,dA}}{ \displaystyle L_x L_y f_0 g \left(\frac{\overline \rho (H)}{\overline N(H)^2 \overline \theta(H) } - \frac{\overline \rho (0)}{\overline N(0)^2 \overline \theta(0) }\right)}
\end{equation}

Experimentally it was found that $\theta^* \sim \qty{-0.1}{\kelvin}$, representing a very small anomaly in potential temperature, smaller than the approximate \qty{-2}{\kelvin} from \cite{sprenger2007userguide}.

To check if \cref{general_solv_cond} is correct, the flux of the linear form can be numerically calculated by comparing how close $L(1)-\alpha(\psi, 1)$ is to zero.
As shown above, $\alpha(\psi, 1)=0$, so only $L(1)$ needs to be evaluated.
This is done by making the substitution $\phi = 1$ and evaluating \mintinline{python}{L} (defined in \cref{app:weak_form}) \cite{UFL}:

\begin{minted}{python}
flux = assemble(replace(L, {phi: Constant(1)}))
\end{minted}

Executing the above expression shows that the net flux across the whole domain is $\sim 10^{-3}$. Given the domain volume of $\sim \qty{e18}{\meter \cubed}$, a net flux of $\sim 10^{-3}$ is on the order of machine precision, confirming that \cref{general_solv_cond} is satisfied up to floating-point truncation error.

\subsection{Solver Settings}\label{sec:solver_settings}

With the equation fully specified in Firedrake's syntax, we can proceed with the solver setup and parameter selection.

Since the coefficients of the bilinear form in \cref{final_weak_form} are time-independent and \cref{qgpv} is linear, the Jacobian remains constant.
Explicitly instructing Firedrake to retain this constant Jacobian\footnote{shown in \cref{app:solver_params}} resulted in significant speed improvements during repeated solves.

As discussed previously, this is a pure Neumann problem, making it necessary to pass a constant nullspace to Firedrake\footnote{shown in \cref{app:solver_params}}.
This prevents the solver from attempting to invert a singular matrix with a rank deficiency of one.

To finally solve the quasigeostrophic potential vorticity equation (\cref{qgpv}), an efficient set of solver parameters must be specified.
A benchmarking search was conducted across over 40 permutations of theoretically reasonable solver parameters on the primary testing hardware (see \cref{app:hardware_consumer}).
Pre-conditioners such as Fast Diagonalisation (the \mintinline{python}{'firedrake.FDMPC'} pre-conditioner \cite{FiredrakeUserManual}) and polynomial multigrid ($p$-multigrid) were evaluated, alongside a wide variety of smoothers and solvers for the coarse $p=1$ mesh.
The performance of matrix-free methods was also compared against assembled matrix methods.
From this reasonably extensive search, the fastest combination of solver parameters was found to be:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Matrix Format:} Matrix-free. The sparse matrix (AIJ format) \cite{PETScUserManual} is never built on the refined levels; instead, each time the solver needs the product of the matrix with a vector, that product is calculated directly from the weak form, cell by cell \cite{FiredrakeUserManual, KirbyMitchell2018}.
    \item \textbf{Krylov Subspace Solver:} The Conjugate Gradient (CG) method. This can be used because it was shown that the matrix arising from the bilinear weak form (\cref{final_weak_form}) is symmetric and positive semi-definite \cite{Saad2003}.
    \item \textbf{Pre-conditioner:} A $p$-multigrid scheme using the \mintinline{python}{'firedrake.PMGPC'} pre-conditioner \cite{FiredrakeUserManual}.
    \item \textbf{Multigrid Smoother:} A Chebyshev iteration, pre-conditioned with point-Jacobi, is applied across the refined multigrid levels ($p=2,3,4$). A Chebyshev smoother is designed to quickly remove the rapidly oscillating (high-frequency) components of the error, which is exactly the job of a smoother. To do so, it only needs an estimate of the matrix's largest eigenvalue \cite{PETScUserManual}. The Jacobi diagonal can be obtained cheaply from a matrix-free operator \cite{FiredrakeUserManual}. The same combination serves as the basic smoother of large-scale matrix-free multigrid solvers \cite{Muench2024}.
    \item \textbf{Coarse Grid Solver:} Only the $p=1$ coarse operator is assembled, using the \mintinline{python}{'firedrake.AssembledPC'} pre-conditioner inside the otherwise matrix-free multigrid hierarchy \cite{FiredrakeUserManual}. A sparse Cholesky factorisation (via MUMPS \cite{PETScUserManual, MUMPS01, MUMPS02}) is conducted exactly once at the first solve, with null-pivot detection enabled to handle the rank deficiency of the matrix. Each subsequent solve using the constant Jacobian is a highly efficient $\mathcal{O}(\text{non-zero entries})$ operation \cite{Davis2006}.
\end{itemize}

A detailed implementation of the solver set-up is given in \cref{app:solver_params}.

\end{figure}

Matrix-free methods are typically faster than assembled matrix methods for $p \geq 2$ \cite{Kronbichler2022}, because memory bandwidth and capacity generally become bottlenecks at these higher orders. %PENISPOINT
Additionally, on 3D hexahedral cells, assembled sparse matrix-vector multiplication requires $\mathcal{O}(p^6)$ operations per cell, whereas matrix-free methods using sum-factorisation reduce the operation count to $\mathcal{O}(p^4)$ per cell \cite{Homolya2017, KirbyMitchell2018}.

These theoretical advantages were verified in practice: experiments involving repeated solutions of \cref{final_weak_form} showed that the matrix-free configuration described above was consistently faster than every assembled configuration tested, on both the primary testing hardware and the HPC cluster (see \cref{app:hardware}).
Matrix-free methods were developed largely because solving massive systems of equations demands significant memory \cite{Muench2024}.
An assembled matrix remains viable only while it is not itself the bottleneck, since every non-zero entry must be loaded from main memory (RAM) at each matrix-vector product \cite{Muench2024}.
On modern computer architectures, fetching data from main memory causes the processor to stall, wasting valuable CPU cycles.
The CPU must wait for data to propagate from RAM through the L3, L2, and L1 caches before finally reaching the registers \cite{hennessy2017computer}.
Sparse matrix-vector multiplication performs roughly $1/6$ floating-point operations per byte loaded, far below the $\approx 10$ at which modern hardware is balanced \cite{KirbyMitchell2018}.
Matrix-free methods aim to minimize these stalls by performing technically redundant or duplicate operations on the fly.
Because modern CPUs can compute operations much faster than memory can supply data, this trade-off is often highly performant \cite{Muench2024}.

It was initially expected that the spatially varying coefficients of \cref{final_weak_form} would erode this advantage.
These coefficients lack an analytical expression (as discussed alongside \cref{nonelementary_integral}), and consequently must be stored in memory.
For a mesh where $N_x=N_y=N_z=35$ and $p=4$, the system has \num{2803221} degrees of freedom (see \cref{degrees_of_freedom}).
Storing the source term (\cref{full_q}) with double precision floating points requires $\qty{8}{\byte} \times \num{2803221} \approx \qty{22}{\mega\byte}$ of memory.
This exceeds the L3 cache capacity of the primary testing hardware (see \cref{app:hardware_consumer}), meaning the source term must be fetched entirely from RAM every time it is evaluated.

However, an assembled matrix must also be fetched from RAM at every matrix-vector product, and it is far larger.
On the same mesh, each row of the $p=4$ matrix holds on average about 212 non-zero entries, giving roughly \num{5.9e8} non-zeros in total\footnote{
Evaluating \mintinline{python}{assemble(a).petscmat.getInfo()["nz_used"] } yields \num{594823321} stored entries, or about 212 per row.
Here \mintinline{python}{a} is the bilinear form defined in \cref{app:weak_form}.
The number of stored entries per row is higher than in \cref{fig:alpha_sparsity}, because a mesh with $N=2$ has only boundary cells which neighbour fewer cells than interior ones (comprising most of an $N=35$ mesh).
}.
Stored in AIJ format with a double-precision value and a four-byte column index per entry \cite{PETScUserManual}, this requires approximately \qty{7}{\giga\byte} of memory, over 300 times that of the source term.
The same disparity has been observed in Firedrake, where assembled three-dimensional operators consume well over an order of magnitude more memory per degree of freedom than their matrix-free equivalents \cite{KirbyMitchell2018}.
Furthermore, even when coefficients are pre-computed and loaded from RAM at every quadrature point, making the matrix-free evaluation memory-bound, its throughput remains more than an order of magnitude higher than that of a sparse matrix-vector product at the same polynomial degree \cite{Kronbichler2022}.

In essence, matrix-free methods typically excel by minimizing slow memory accesses \cite{Muench2024}.
Although our specific application must fetch its coefficient fields from RAM regardless, this traffic is small compared with streaming an assembled high-order sparse matrix.
As a result, matrix-free methods outperform fully assembled sparse matrices in this context, and assembly is best reserved for the small $p=1$ coarse operator, where it enables an exact direct solve.

Nevertheless, the following caveats should be noted:
\begin{itemize}
    \item
    Assembling the matrix costs approximately ten matrix-free operator applications \cite{KirbyMitchell2018}.
    This cost was expected to be recovered over repeated solves with the constant Jacobian, but in practice each assembled solve remained slower than its matrix-free equivalent, so the cost was never recovered.

    \item
    The memory footprint of the matrix-free solver is substantially smaller than that of the assembled alternative.
    Only the $p=1$ coarse matrix is stored (approximately \num{1.2e6} non-zeros, or \qty{14}{\mega\byte} before Cholesky fill-in, for the mesh above), which permits far larger meshes on consumer-grade hardware (see \cref{app:solver_params}).

    \item
    A transformation for \cref{qgpv} might exist that absorbs the spatially varying coefficients into $\psi$ itself, or into a rescaled coordinates $x',y',z'$.
    Such a formulation would remove the remaining coefficient memory traffic, potentially widening the performance gap in favour of matrix-free methods further.

    \item
    On systems with abundant L1 and L2 cache, such as HPC clusters with dozens of cores per node, the performance gap between matrix-free and assembled methods was expected to narrow.
    Repeating the benchmark on such a cluster (see \cref{app:hardware_hpc}) confirmed that the matrix-free configuration remained the fastest.

    \item
    This advantage relies on sum-factorisation, which applies only to tensor-product cells \cite{Homolya2017}.
    On simplicial meshes, where Firedrake did not offer sum-factorisation, assembled sparse matrix-vector products were found to be faster than matrix-free ones \cite{KirbyMitchell2018}, so these conclusions should not be assumed to transfer to tetrahedral meshes.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Solver Verification}\label{sec:mms}

To verify the solver itself is correct, the method of manufactured solutions will be used \cite{Roache2002}.
We choose some analytically expressible function $\psi_a(x,y,z)$ that has similar properties to the true solution to \cref{qgpv}.
The source term and boundary conditions that arise from $\psi_a$ can then be directly calculated.
These boundary conditions and source term are then provided to the numerical solver we defined above.
If the solver's numerical solution matches $\psi_a$ to some high degree of precision, the solver is likely correctly implemented.

The domain, mesh and all other parameters are left at their control values, but the coefficient profiles of \cref{qgpv} are simplified so that the source term stays short enough to write down:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Constant reference stability profile: $\overline{N}(z)=N=\qty{0.01}{\per\second}$ \cite{Vallis2017}
    \item An exponential density profile $\overline{\rho}(z)=\rho_0 \exp (-z/H_\rho)$, with $\rho_0 = \qty{1.225}{\kilo\gram\per\meter\cubed}$ and $H_\rho = \qty{8.5}{\km}$ \cite{Stull2015}
\end{itemize}

Equation \eqref{qgpv} then reduces to a simple advection-diffusion equation with constant coefficients:

\begin{equation}\label{mms_source}
    q = \frac{\partial ^2 \psi_a}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial ^2 \psi_a}{\partial y^2} + \frac{f_0^2}{N^2} \left( \frac{\partial ^2 \psi_a}{\partial z^2} - \frac{1}{H_\rho} \frac{\partial \psi_a}{\partial z}\right)
\end{equation}

The manufactured solution is built from the three structures the true solution possesses -- the jet stream, the response to the PV anomaly of \cref{Q_star}, and the vertically uniform $\theta^*$ adjustment of \cref{sec:solv_cond}:

\begin{equation}\label{mms_psi}
    \psi_a(x,y,z) = \overline{\psi}(y,z)
    + \Psi_a \left[1 + \frac{\left(x - x_\text{ano}\right)^2 + \left(y - y_\text{ano}\right)^2}{L_a^2} + \left(\frac{z - z_a}{h_a}\right)^2\right]^{-1}
    + \Lambda z
\end{equation}

Here $\overline{\psi}$ is the background streamfunction of \cref{psi_bar} at the control jet parameters, and the anomaly term is centred on the control anomaly position $(x_\text{ano}, y_\text{ano}) = (L_x/2, L_y/2)$.
The anomaly term is a Lorentzian rather than a Gaussian because inverting \cref{qgpv} spreads the Gaussian source of \cref{Q_star} into a profile with far broader tails.
A least-squares fit against the numerical solution at the control parameters gives $\Psi_a = \qty{8e6}{\meter\squared\per\second}$, $L_a = \qty{500}{\km}$, $h_a = \qty{5}{\km}$, $z_a = \qty{8.75}{\km}$ and $\Lambda = \qty{45}{\meter\per\second}$, at which \cref{mms_psi} is within \qty{3}{\percent} of that solution in the relative $L^2$ norm.
The solver is therefore verified against a problem carrying the same length scales, gradients and magnitudes as the one it is built to solve.
Substituting \cref{mms_psi} into \cref{mms_source} supplies the source term, and its first derivatives supply the boundary data: $\overline v = \partial \psi_a / \partial x$ and $\overline u = -\partial \psi_a / \partial y$ in \cref{qgpv_bcs_hor}, while $\partial \psi_a / \partial z$ is prescribed directly on the top and bottom boundaries in place of \cref{qgpv_bcs_vert}.
Since $\psi$ is only determined up to an additive constant, the mean of each field is removed before the relative $L^2$ error between the numerical solution and $\psi_a$ is taken (\cref{app:rel_error}).

\begin{figure}[tp]
    \centering

Error is calculated using the method from \cref{app:rel_error} with $H=\qty{20}{\kilo\meter}$. Each line's slope is within 0.03 of $p+1$, so $\mathcal{O}\left({\Delta z}^{p+1}\right)$ error convergence is achieved.}
    \label{fig:error_convergence}

The relative error of the numerical solution for varying $p$ against \cref{mms_psi} is given in \cref{fig:error_convergence}.
Since the error of the $p=4$ scheme is satisfactory and the solver settings were optimised for $p=4$, we will keep the arbitrarily chosen $p=4$.
It should also be noted that according to \cref{fig:error_convergence}, this 5th order scheme should provide orders of magnitude less error than the second order scheme used by Barnes et Al, at a fraction of the degrees of freedom.

\subsection{Limitations of QGPV}\label{sec:limits_of_qgpv}

Now that all the numerical tools are in place to set up, compute and plot solutions to \cref{qgpv}, we are able to examine the effect of various atmospheric and anomaly parameters on surface cyclogenesis.

Before proceeding, we must examine the primary limitations of QGPV and EPV, so that we may restrict our parameter search space not only to physically possible states, but also to states in which QGPV inversion remains a reasonable approximation of the true atmospheric state.

The first constraint is on the sign of the EPV.
For full EPV inversion, the requirement $fQ>0$ makes the inversion problem elliptic and well posed, and is equivalent to the balanced state being statically, inertially and symmetrically stable \cite{Hoskins1985}.
To first order, QGPV is related to EPV by linearising about a uniformly stratified reference state at rest, \cite{fehlmann1997pvelements}, although this relation holds only for small relative anomalies and not in general \cite{Hoskins1985}.
Under this relation $fQ>0$ is equivalent to $q/f > -1$; the QGPV inversion operator itself is linear and elliptic for any $q$, so this is a condition for the inverted state to be physically realisable rather than for the inversion to be solvable.
We set up a direct way to quantify this in \cref{V_minus}, which brings the implication that for any atmosphere to be physically valid, $\mathcal{V}_-=0$.
As described in \cref{sec:source_term}, $\mathcal{V}_-$ is driven largely by $|\partial \overline u / \partial z|$.

A further but looser constraint is placed on the Rossby number.
QGPV is only formally valid $\mathrm{Ro} \ll 1$, although it remains qualitatively useful for observed disturbance amplitudes \cite{HOLTON_chapter6}.
Davis identified the inaccuracy of QG as the Rossby number becomes $\mathcal{O}(1)$ as its main weakness, while noting, following earlier work, that it may still give useful qualitative information in such situations \cite{Davis1992jas}.
Davis did not directly measure the error of QG inversion against full EPV inversion, but did compare a truncated linear piecewise inversion, which nearly reduces to QG for a mean state at rest, with the full linear piecewise method \cite{Davis1992jas}.
For an anomaly with maximum vorticity $\zeta_{\max}  = \qty{1.9e-4}{\per\second}$ (Ro $\approx 1.9$ for $f \approx \qty{e-4}{\per\second}$), the maximum surface wind attributed to one third of the anomaly differed between these methods by approximately 12\%, rising to 24\% at $\zeta_{\max} \approx \qty{2.9e-4}{\per\second}$ \cite{Davis1992jas}.
Davis further found that the differences among these methods were smaller than their differences from QG inversion \cite{Davis1992jas}, so these figures should be read as a lower bound on the error of QG.
For positive anomalies, the near-QG method produced stronger winds than the full linear method at the level of the anomaly, but weaker winds at the surface \cite{Davis1992jas}.
This is consistent with vertical penetration of the anomaly increasing with its amplitude, whereas QG predicts a fixed penetration depth \cite{Davis1992jas}, so QG will tend to underestimate the surface response to strong upper-level anomalies.
It must also be noted that Muraki et al.\ report that, at $\mathrm{Ro} \approx 0.3$, next-order corrections to QG remove qualitative deficiencies and significantly improve quantitative accuracy in the time-evolution of a baroclinic wave on a jet \cite{Muraki1999}.
QGPV therefore already carries noticeable errors well below $\mathrm{Ro} = 1$.
Unfortunately further investigating the error of QGPV inversion is beyond the reasonable scope of this Applied Mathematics Honours project.

A restriction is additionally placed on the Froude number (\cref{froude_number}), which must be much less than one and comparable to $\mathrm{Ro}$.
Finally, EPV is conserved following the flow only for adiabatic, frictionless motion \cite{Hoskins1985}.
When condensation or evaporation occurs, heat is added to or removed from the air, and PV is no longer conserved.
The inversion itself is instantaneous and does not require adiabatic flow, but moisture still affects it: condensation in rising air reduces the effective static stability, which increases the vertical penetration of an anomaly and enhances surface development \cite{Hoskins1985}.
Our inversion uses dry stratification everywhere, so it may underestimate the surface response in moist ascending regions, the same direction of bias as the QG penetration error above.

Sweeps of atmospheric and anomaly parameters must apply these restrictions, so as to limit the chance of non-physical results being derived.

\subsection{One Parameter at a Time}\label{sec:one_param}

Varying a single parameter at a time, using the control state as a base, allows us to investigate the surface effects of each parameter in isolation.
For this experiment we do not place any restrictions on $\mathcal{V}_-$ , $\mathrm{Ro}$ or $\mathrm{Fr}$.
We simply aim to see how each parameter in \cref{tab:parameter_bounds} affects maximum surface wind speed, minimum dynamical tropopause height, minimum surface pressure anomaly and minimum surface vorticity.

For each parameter $g$ in \cref{tab:parameter_bounds} that varies in the range $g\in[a,b]$, we define a $g(x)=a + (b-a)x$, with $x\in[0,1]$.
This gives a normalised parameter range so that each parameter may be plotted on the same set of axes.

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{maximum_surface_wind_speed.pdf}
    \caption{Effect of taking the control state and varying a single parameter at a time on maximum surface wind speed.}
    \label{fig:max_surf_wind}
\end{figure}

It can be seen in \cref{fig:max_surf_wind} that surface wind has a trivial and potentially non-physical relationship with $z_\text{trop}$.
The Gaussian jet stream (\cref{u_boundary}) induces its own wind upon the surface once $z_\text{jet}$ is large enough, and thus the surface wind is not caused by the anomaly.
This is supported by the exponential profile of the $z_\text{jet}$ line in \cref{fig:max_surf_wind}, following the exponential increase of effectively moving the Gaussian closer.
This effect also appears to occur in \cref{fig:min_surf_pres_ano}, increasing the magnitude of the surface pressure anomaly by inducing baroclinity via non-cyclonic wind.

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{minimum_surface_pressure_anomaly.pdf}
    \caption{Effect of taking the control state and varying a single parameter at a time on minimum surface pressure anomaly.}
    \label{fig:min_surf_pres_ano}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{minimum_surface_vorticity.pdf}
    \caption{Effect of taking the control state and varying a single parameter at a time on minimum surface vorticity.}
    \label{fig:min_surf_vort}
\end{figure}

Comparing \cref{fig:min_surf_vort} with \cref{fig:min_surf_pres_ano,fig:max_surf_wind} reveals that anomaly size affects surface wind and the pressure anomaly much more than minimum surface vorticity.
This agrees with the findings of Barnes et al. \cite{Barnes2022main}.
Moreover, \cref{fig:min_surf_vort,fig:min_surf_pres_ano,fig:max_surf_wind} show that a weakly stratified troposphere moderately intensifies surface effects, even within physically plausible ranges established in \cref{tab:parameter_bounds}.

Jet stream parameters are what determine $\partial \overline{u}/ \partial z$ and hence drive $\mathcal{V}_-$ away from 0, presenting difficulties in justifying the validity of QGPV inversion in certain regions of the parameter space.
Interestingly however, $U_\text{jet}$, $L_\text{jet}$ and $y_\text{jet}$ have almost no effect on the surface, which may justify using weaker jet streams (or potentially none at all) when performing parameter sweeps, further allowable by the linearity of \cref{qgpv}.
Expectedly due to linearity, the surface vorticity (\cref{fig:min_surf_vort}), the jet has no effect at all.

Increasing latitude consequently increases $f$, and thereby increases the magnitude of the stretching term in \cref{qgpv}, likely inducing the slight poleward bias towards the stronger surface effects visible in \cref{fig:min_surf_vort,fig:min_surf_pres_ano,fig:max_surf_wind}.

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{minimum_dynamical_tropopause_height.pdf}
    \caption{Effect of taking the control state and varying a single parameter at a time on minimum dynamical tropopause height. Step-like behaviour is due to $z_\text{dyn}$ only being resolved to the nearest degree of freedom, and is hence discrete.}
    \label{fig:min_dyn_trop}
\end{figure}

A strange and unexplained relationship is the sharp step of the $y_\text{jet}$ line in \cref{fig:min_dyn_trop}.
As the jet is offset further poleward from the anomaly, $z_\text{dyn}$ exhibits a slight decrease.
However at the normalised parameter value of 0.6, $z_\text{dyn}$ is sharply pulled up to a \qty{10}{\kilo\meter} altitude.
This corresponds to $y_\text{jet} \approx L_y/2 - \qty{300}{\kilo\meter}$.
The phenomenon may be related to $L_\text{jet} = \qty{500}{\kilo\meter}$ in the control parameters, but no explanation is offered at present.

\Cref{fig:max_surf_wind,fig:min_dyn_trop,fig:min_surf_pres_ano,fig:min_surf_vort} show common behaviour, shedding some light on which atmospheric and anomaly parameters most significantly affect the surface across multiple metrics.
The tropopause thickness parameters $w_\text{trop}$ and $\delta$ have almost no measurable effect on any of the plots, most surprisingly \cref{fig:min_dyn_trop}.
This is reassuring as it shows that how the tropopause is treated numerically is not important when calculating surface effects.
The same is true for $\overline{N}'_\text{strat}$, which could be why Barnes et al. use an unrealistic value for $\overline{N}_\text{strat}$ \cite{Barnes2022main}; it simply does not matter on the surface.
Furthermore, the aspect ratio of the anomaly $y_\text{size}/x_\text{size}$ unexpectedly has no effect on the surface wind, vorticity or pressure anomaly.
Surface temperature variation $\overline{T}'(0)$ also has very little to no effect on any of the plots, with the exception of causing slight variation in $z_\text{dyn}$ in \cref{fig:min_dyn_trop}.

In accordance with the findings of Barnes et al., $z_\text{trop}$ and $z_\text{size}$ have a significant effect on all three surface quantities \cite{Barnes2022main}, and have a trivially strong relationship with $z_\text{dyn}$.

Of \cref{fig:max_surf_wind,fig:min_dyn_trop,fig:min_surf_pres_ano,fig:min_surf_vort}, only \cref{fig:min_dyn_trop} appears to show a non-negligible effect induced by $Q_\text{anoclip}$.
This is likely caused in part by its range being much smaller than most of the other parameters.

Barnes et al. \cite{Barnes2022main} found that the minimum dynamical tropopause height is the major factor in surface cyclogenetic forcing, more so than the intrusion's vertical extent.
We can view the relationship between the surface variables and minimum dynamical tropopause height by reusing the data from \cref{fig:max_surf_wind,fig:min_dyn_trop,fig:min_surf_pres_ano,fig:min_surf_vort}.

\begin{figure}[hbtp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{tropopause_height_correlations.pdf}
    \caption{Minimum dynamical tropopause height versus minimum surface pressure anomaly, maximum surface wind speed and minimum surface vorticity. No restrictions are placed on $\mathcal{V}_-$, $\mathrm{Ro}$ or $\mathrm{Fr}$. Parameters are varied one at a time from the control parameters.}
    \label{fig:trop_height_correl}
\end{figure}

Most of the observations above arising from \cref{fig:max_surf_wind,fig:min_dyn_trop,fig:min_surf_pres_ano,fig:min_surf_vort} are also apparent in \cref{fig:trop_height_correl}.
The parameters that increase surface effects are also correlated with a lowered $z_\text{dyn}$.
One of the noticeable exceptions is $\overline{N}_\text{trop}$.
$\overline{N}_\text{trop}$ brings \textit{up} $z_\text{dyn}$ while increasing surface effects.
Another exception is anomaly size.
Anomaly size does not affect $z_\text{dyn}$, but has a major effect on $p^*_{z=0}$ and $|\bm{u}|_{z=0}$.
A wider anomaly makes the surface low deeper, but it also spreads the low over a larger area.
Those two effects largely cancel in the vorticity, while pressure and wind keep growing \cite{Barnes2022main}.

Since no restrictions are placed on whether each parameter evaluation results in a QG-invertible or physically plausible ($\mathcal{V}_-=0$) atmosphere, these findings may not hold in full EPV inversion.
Furthermore, nonlinear relationships between parameters are not possible to investigate by varying a single parameter at a time.

\subsection{Random Sampling}\label{sec:random_sampling}

In an effort to address the issues stated above, we investigate the relationship between the surface variables and $z_\text{dyn}$ by randomly and uniformly sampling parameter vectors from the ranges outlined in \cref{tab:parameter_bounds}.
We reject any samples with $\mathcal{V}_- > 0$, and colour each sample point by its Rossby number.
Accumulating 1000 valid samples gives \cref{fig:random_sample}.
Only 5\% of parameter vectors sampled meet the $\mathcal{V}_- = 0$ requirement, raising doubts as to the validity of the findings of \cref{sec:one_param}.

\begin{figure}[hbtp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{random_sample_trop_correlations.pdf}
    \caption{1000 randomly sampled parameters from \cref{tab:parameter_bounds} with $\mathcal{V}_- = 0$. Points corresponding to an individual atmospheric state are coloured by their Rossby number.}
    \label{fig:random_sample}
\end{figure}

We employ the Spearman $\rho$ to quantify the strength of the monotonic relationship between each surface variable and $z_\text{dyn}$.
\Cref{fig:random_sample} shows a moderately strong monotonic relationship between $z_\text{dyn}$ and the surface variables, with vorticity being on the edge of what could be called a strong relationship \cite{Schober2018}.

However, most of the atmospheric states in \cref{fig:random_sample} are beyond the range of $\mathrm{Ro}$ for which QGPV is quantitatively or qualitatively useful (as discussed in \cref{sec:limits_of_qgpv}).
To address this we drop any points with $\mathrm{Ro} \geq 1$ to produce \cref{fig:random_sample_ro}.

\begin{figure}[hbtp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{random_sample_trop_correlations_ro_below_1.pdf}
    \caption{Same as \cref{fig:random_sample}, but only the 21.6\% of points with $\mathrm{Ro} < 1$.}
    \label{fig:random_sample_ro}
\end{figure}

\Cref{fig:random_sample_ro} now only consists of points which fall broadly within the range that QGPV has (at minimum) qualitative usefulness.
For quantitative usefulness, we require $\mathrm{Ro} \lesssim 0.5$, but only 0.6\% of points have $\mathrm{Ro} < 0.5$, so this was practical to test.
The $\rho$ of each panel changes very little from \cref{fig:random_sample} to \cref{fig:random_sample_ro}, which supports the moderate monotonic relationship between $z_\text{dyn}$ and surface effects.

The above findings support the conclusion that minimum surface vorticity, minimum surface pressure anomaly and maximum surface wind speed are largely driven by the minimum dynamical tropopause height induced by an anomaly.
Other characteristics of the anomaly are not as important as the height of the \qty{-1.5}{PVU} contour above the surface.

\subsection{Maximising Surface Response}

Automating the search for parameters that maximise surface response requires setting up an optimisation problem.
We first reduce the dimensionality of the problem by choosing to not vary $w_\text{trop}$, $\delta$ and $\overline{N}'_\text{strat}$, as \cref{sec:one_param} shows that they have negligible effect on the surface.
Using the parameter normalisation scheme from \cref{sec:one_param}, we represent any given choice of parameters as a vector $\bm{x} \in [0,1]^d$, where $d$ denotes the dimensionality or number of parameters we allow to vary.
Computing derivatives of $\min p^*_{z=0}$ and $\min -|\bm{u}|_{z=0}$ with respect to each of the parameters in \cref{tab:parameter_bounds} is non-trivial due to the highly nonlinear relationships between some parameters and the surface variables.
For example, one of the chains of dependence goes as $\min p^*_{z=0} \to p^* \to \psi \to  q \to  Q^* \to z_\text{size}$, where the arrows each represent complex or nonlinear dependence relations.
This rules out any optimsation schemes which require gradients, such as variants of gradient descent.

Instead we opt for a derivative-free bounded optimiser.
One such method is BOBYQA \cite{Powell2009}, implemented in Python as Py-BOBYQA \cite{Cartis2019}.
It can be configured to seek the global minimum, as opposed to a local one \cite{Cartis2021}, which improves the exhaustiveness of our parameter sweep.
We begin the implementation by defining a cost function we seek to minimise:

\begin{equation}\label{cost}
    C(\bm{x}) = \frac{O}{\sqrt{\tilde{\mathrm{Ro}}\tilde{\mathrm{Fr}}}} + \mu \mathcal{V}_-
\end{equation}

Here $O$ is the objective we wish to minimise, either $\min -|\bm{u}|_{z=0}$ or $\min p^*_{z=0}$.
The penalty parameter which drives $\mathcal{V}_- \to 0$ is $\mu=10^6$, quickly pushing the optimiser away from parameters that cause $\mathcal{V}_- > 0$.
The denominator rewards small Rossby and Froude numbers, and punishes large ones, telling the solver to find a balance between surface effect and QG validity.
Large $\mathrm{Ro}, \mathrm{Fr} > 1$ push $C(\bm{x})$ closer to zero, when interesting values of $O$ are $\sim -10$.
We use clipped $\tilde{\mathrm{Ro}} = \max\left\{\mathrm{Ro}_\text{max}, 0.5\right\}$ and  $\tilde{\mathrm{Fr}} = \max\left\{\mathrm{Fr}_\text{max}, 0.5\right\}$ to prevent the optimiser from finding that sending $\mathrm{Ro} \times\mathrm{Fr} \to 0$ is the most effective way to send $C(\bm{x}) \to -\infty$, thereby polluting the results.
We configure Py-BOBYQA to run on a single MPI rank (see \cref{app:mpi}), while allowing the inversion of \cref{qgpv} to run on all ranks.

Running the optimiser for both objectives -- maximum surface wind speed and minimum surface pressure anomaly -- yields \cref{fig:optimiser}.
Py-BOBYQA is configured to call $C(\bm{x})$ a maximum of 5000 times in each case.

\begin{figure}[tp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{optimiser_cost.pdf}
    \caption{Convergence of Py-BOBYQA towards the global minimum of $C(\bm{x})$, for both objectives $O$.}
    \label{fig:optimiser}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Numerical Vertical Integrator}\label{app:vert_int}

Firedrake does not provide a simple way to evaluate integrals of the form:

\begin{equation}
    I(z) = \int_0^z f(\xi) \,d\xi
\end{equation}

$I(z)$ could be solved with Firedrake by rewriting it as an initial value problem (IVP):

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial I}{\partial z} = f(z), \quad I(0)=0
\end{equation}

On our 3D mesh defined in \cref{sec:mesh}, we would be wasting an enormous amount of CPU time and memory by repeatedly solving an identical IVP in every vertical column, when the integrand depends exclusively on $z$.
Additionally, the IVP consists of only advection, which is unsuitable for the \mintinline{python}{'Q'} function space we chose in \cref{sec:cg_space}.

To circumvent this the \mintinline{python}{'DQ'} function space could be implemented, but this would require expensive projections back and forth between both function spaces \cite{FiredrakeUserManual}.
A more efficient approach would be to make use of the pre-existing high-order numerical integration system in the package \mintinline{python}{scipy} \cite{scipy}, and then write the result back into a Firedrake \mintinline{python}{Function}.
The annotated code that performs the integration up to 4th order accuracy is given:

\begin{minted}{python}
def compute_vertical_integral(integrand, cg_space):
    """
    Computes the vertical integral I(z) = \int_{z_{bottom}}^z f(\xi) d\xi.
    
    Collapses the mesh into a single 1D vertical profile. The integration is handled by SciPy's CubicSpline, which maintains high-order accuracy compatible with higher-degree Firedrake function spaces, before mapping the result back to a 3D Firedrake Function.
    """
    mesh = cg_space.mesh()

    # Extract Z-coordinates and integrand values at all mesh Degrees of Freedom (DoFs)
    z_expr = SpatialCoordinate(mesh)[2]

    z_fd = Function(cg_space).interpolate(z_expr)
    f_fd = Function(cg_space).interpolate(integrand)

    z_data = z_fd.dat.data_ro
    f_data = f_fd.dat.data_ro

    # Sort DoFs vertically. SciPy's CubicSpline requires strictly increasing coordinates.
    sort_idx = np.argsort(z_data)
    z_sorted = z_data[sort_idx]
    f_sorted = f_data[sort_idx]

    # Collapse 3D mesh into a 1D vertical profile. This prevents redundant integration across identical horizontal columns and removes duplicate Z-coordinates that would crash the spline.
    z_rounded = np.round(z_sorted, decimals=8)
    _, unique_idx = np.unique(z_rounded, return_index=True)

    z_1d = z_sorted[unique_idx]
    f_1d = f_sorted[unique_idx]

    # Fit a continuous piecewise cubic polynomial to the 1D profile
    spline = CubicSpline(z_1d, f_1d)

    # Analytically integrate the spline (producing a 4th-degree polynomial)
    integral_spline = spline.antiderivative()

    # Evaluate the integral back at the original 3D mesh DoF coordinates.
    # Subtracting the bottom boundary value enforces the condition I(0) = 0.
    integral_dofs = integral_spline(z_data) - integral_spline(z_1d[0])

    # Write the 1D evaluated data back into a native Firedrake Function
    result = Function(cg_space, name="Vertical_Integral")
    result.dat.data[:] = integral_dofs

    return result
\end{minted}

When running with MPI, this function does not collate the data between ranks onto a single rank.
This means that each rank evaluates the integral only on the data in its subset of the mesh.
If the mesh were to be subdivided vertically, this would completely break the function and
incorrect results would be given.
However, Firedrake only subdivides extruded meshes horizontally \cite{FiredrakeUserManual, LangeMitchellKnepleyGorman2015, PTSCOTCH}, so each MPI rank is operating on its own independent vertical column.

For this reason, the \mintinline{python}{compute_vertical_integral} function will \textbf{only} produce accurate results for \textbf{extruded meshes}.
This results in a slight increase in efficiency as communication between ranks is not necessary.

\subsection{Weak Form}\label{app:weak_form}

Equation \eqref{final_weak_form} is expressed in Firedrake's syntax, which is based on Unified Form Language (UFL) \cite{UFL}.
This code is included as certain parts of the syntax can be quite unintuitive, especially with extruded meshes.

\begin{minted}{python}
# Define the trial and test functions
psi = TrialFunction(cg_space)
phi = TestFunction(cg_space)
n = FacetNormal(mesh) # The normal vector n=[n_x, n_y, n_z]

# The value of the below expression will be evaluated at both vertical boundaries
vertical_boundary = phys_params.g * theta_star / (phys_params.f * theta_bar)

# Calculate the quotient in advance
# Prevents huge quadrature degrees from being estimated by the form compiler if a quadrature degree is not explicitly set
rho_N2 = Function(cg_space).interpolate(rho_bar / (N_bar**2))

x_base = rho_bar * phi * v * n[0]
y_base = rho_bar * phi * -u * n[1]
z_base = n[2] * (f**2) * phi * rho_N2

L_x = x_base * ds_v((1, 2)) # Tuple that refers to both x-boundaries on the extruded mesh
L_y = y_base * ds_v((3, 4)) # Same but for y-boundaries
L_z = z_base * self._vertical_boundary * ds_tb # ds_bt represents the vertical boundaries in an extruded mesh
L_vol = -q * rho_bar * phi * dx

# Linear form L
L = L_x + L_y + L_z + L_vol

# Bilinear form alpha
a = (rho_bar * psi.dx(0) * phi.dx(0) +
     rho_bar * psi.dx(1) * phi.dx(1) +
     (f**2) * rho_N2 * psi.dx(2) * phi.dx(2)) * dx
\end{minted}

An important detail to note is that constants that may change depending on atmospheric parameters (such as \mintinline{python}{phys_params.f}) should be wrapped with \mintinline{python}{Constant(...)} once, and subsequently updated with its \mintinline{python}{assign()} method.
If a plain Python float is used instead, its value is embedded in the UFL form as a literal, so each new parameter value produces a different form, for which Firedrake must generate and compile new C kernels \cite{FiredrakeUserManual}.
A \mintinline{python}{Constant} is instead a symbolic coefficient whose value is passed to the already compiled kernel at run time, so the form, and hence the generated code, is reused across parameter changes.

\subsection{Running in Parallel with MPI}\label{app:mpi}

To run Firedrake in parallel, it is necessary to use \href{https://www.firedrakeproject.org/parallelism.html}{a Message Passing Interface (MPI) Library} \cite{FiredrakeUserManual, Dalcin2011}.
For example:

\begin{minted}{bash}
mpiexec -n 16 python simulation.py
\end{minted}

It is worth noting that standard python functions will run redundantly on every single MPI rank, including \mintinline{python}{print()} statements.
Firedrake's standard functions know how to handle multiple ranks seamlessly, however normal python libraries will not necessarily handle this correctly.
Instead Firedrake's functions should be used where possible, such as \mintinline{python}{PETSc.Sys.Print()} instead of \mintinline{python}{print()} \cite{FiredrakeUserManual, Dalcin2011}.

\subsection{Relative Error Norm}\label{app:rel_error}

Because \cref{qgpv} is a pure Neumann problem, the numerical solution $\psi_h$ can only be compared with the manufactured solution $\psi_a$ up to an additive constant.
The constant chosen is the one that minimises $\lVert \psi_h - \psi_a - c \rVert_{L^2}$, which is the mean of the difference, $c = \langle \psi_h - \psi_a \rangle$, where

\begin{equation}
    \langle g \rangle \equiv \frac{1}{\lvert V\rvert} \int_V g \,dV, \quad \lVert g \rVert_{L^2} \equiv \left( \int_V g^2 \,dV \right)^{1/2}
\end{equation}

The relative error is then:

\begin{equation}\label{rel_error}
    e = \frac{\lVert (\psi_h - \psi_a) - \langle \psi_h - \psi_a \rangle \rVert_{L^2}}{\lVert \psi_a - \langle \psi_a \rangle \rVert_{L^2}}
\end{equation}

The mean is also removed from $\psi_a$ in the denominator, since its additive constant is just as arbitrary and would otherwise inflate $\lVert \psi_a \rVert_{L^2}$ and shrink $e$.

Since $\psi_a$ is not a polynomial, the integrals in \cref{rel_error} cannot be computed exactly.
The quadrature degree is therefore set well above that of the finite element space, so that the quadrature error is not what limits the measured error.
The annotated code, simplified to compute only the $L^2$ norm, is given:

\begin{minted}{python}
def relative_error(exact, numerical : Function):
    """
    Relative L2 error between an exact UFL expression and a numerical Function, after the mean of each is removed.
    """
    # Quadrature degree well above that of the finite element space, so that integrating against the non-polynomial exact solution does not set the error floor
    degree = 3 * numerical.function_space().ufl_element().embedded_superdegree + 6
    dx_q = dx(domain=numerical.function_space().mesh(), degree=degree)

    volume = assemble(Constant(1) * dx_q)

    # The solution is only defined up to an additive constant, so remove the mean
    def mean_removed(expr):
        return expr - assemble(expr * dx_q) / volume

    def l2_norm(expr):
        return np.sqrt(assemble(expr**2 * dx_q))

    return float(l2_norm(mean_removed(numerical - exact)) / l2_norm(mean_removed(exact)))
\end{minted}

Each \mintinline{python}{assemble} call sums its integral over every MPI rank, so unlike \mintinline{python}{compute_vertical_integral}, this function gives the same result however the mesh is partitioned.

\subsection{Lateral Boundary Mean}\label{app:psi_0}

Firedrake has no built-in way to integrate over a curve at each height, as \cref{psi_0} requires.
Instead, the test function is integrated over the lateral walls of the domain, which gives one weight per DoF that is zero away from the walls.
The basis functions of the \mintinline{python}{'Q'} space are products of a horizontal and a vertical part, so every DoF on a given $z$ level shares the same vertical factor in its weight.
That factor cancels when the weighted sum of $\psi$ on a level is divided by the sum of the weights, which leaves exactly the perimeter mean of $\psi_h$ at that level.
The annotated code is given:

\begin{minted}{python}
def compute_psi_0(psi : Function):
    """
    Computes psi_0(z), the mean of psi around the lateral boundary at each height, as a Function that varies with z alone.
    """
    V = psi.function_space()
    mesh = V.mesh()

    # Height of every DoF on this rank, including the halo
    z = Function(V).interpolate(SpatialCoordinate(mesh)[2]).dat.data_ro_with_halos

    # Collapse the heights into the distinct z levels of the extruded mesh
    z_sorted = np.sort(z)
    tol = 1e-8 * (z_sorted[-1] - z_sorted[0])
    levels = z_sorted[np.concatenate(([True], np.diff(z_sorted) > tol))]

    # Index of the level each DoF sits on
    above = np.clip(np.searchsorted(levels, z), 1, levels.size - 1)
    level = np.where(z - levels[above - 1] <= levels[above] - z, above - 1, above)

    # Integrate each basis function over the lateral walls (ds_v); zero for DoFs off the walls
    weights = assemble(TestFunction(V) * ds_v).dat.data_ro
    psi_data = psi.dat.data_ro
    owned_level = level[:weights.size] # data_ro holds only owned DoFs, so none are counted twice

    # Sum the weighted psi, and the weights, on each level
    local = np.concatenate((
        np.bincount(owned_level, weights=weights * psi_data, minlength=levels.size),
        np.bincount(owned_level, weights=weights, minlength=levels.size)
    ))

    # The lateral walls are split between ranks, so add up every rank's contribution
    totals = np.empty_like(local)
    mesh.comm.Allreduce(local, totals, op=MPI.SUM)

    # Weighted mean on each level
    profile = totals[:levels.size] / totals[levels.size:]

    # Write the same profile into every column, so psi_0 is exactly horizontally uniform
    psi_0 = Function(V, name="psi_0")
    psi_0.dat.data_with_halos[:] = profile[level]

    return psi_0
\end{minted}

As with \mintinline{python}{compute_vertical_integral}, this relies on Firedrake only subdividing extruded meshes horizontally, so that every rank holds whole columns, sees every $z$ level, and numbers the levels identically.
Unlike it, the lateral walls are shared between many ranks, so the per-level sums must be combined with an explicit \mintinline{python}{Allreduce} before the mean is taken.
Writing the profile directly into the DoFs, rather than interpolating an expression, guarantees that $\psi_0$ has no horizontal variation, so none can leak into $p^*$ through \cref{p_star}.
